The French Revolution Explained Simply

Written by Viktor Jonas · Last checked on 24 October 2026 · Reading time about 10 minutes

On 14 July 1789, an angry Parisian crowd stormed a fortress that was, by then, of little real military importance - and in doing so set off an event still regarded today as the birth of modern democracy. The French Revolution did not just end centuries of absolute monarchy in France; with its Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen, it also provided the template for countless later constitutions around the world. At the same time, its most radical phase, the Reign of Terror, showed just how quickly revolutionary ideals can turn into violence. It is precisely this double nature - liberation and terror at once - that makes the Revolution one of the most debated topics in European history.

The causes: estates, bankruptcy and hunger

Several causes converged in 1789. Politically, France was locked into a rigid system of estates: the clergy (First Estate) and the nobility (Second Estate) held numerous privileges and were largely exempt from taxes, while the Third Estate - ranging from poor peasants to wealthy merchants - carried almost the entire tax burden yet had almost no political say. Financially, the state was close to bankruptcy: expensive wars, above all France's support for the American independence movement against Britain (1778-1783), had emptied the treasury, without the tax privileges of the nobility and clergy ever being touched. Intellectually, Enlightenment ideas - popular sovereignty, the separation of powers, and human rights, as articulated by thinkers such as Rousseau and Montesquieu - prepared the ground for challenging the king's divine right to rule. And very concretely, disastrous harvests in 1788 and 1789 sent bread prices soaring and caused widespread hunger - the immediate trigger of the crisis.

The Estates-General and the National Assembly

In May 1789, King Louis XVI convened the Estates-General for the first time in 175 years, an assembly of the three estates meant to discuss new taxes. When the Third Estate found itself disadvantaged by the traditional system of voting by order rather than by head, it declared itself, on its own authority, the National Assembly on 17 June 1789 - the true representative of the French people. When the king responded by locking the assembly hall, the deputies moved to a nearby indoor tennis court and swore the famous Tennis Court Oath there on 20 June 1789: they would not separate until France had a constitution. This moment marks the true beginning of the Revolution as an organized political movement.

From the Third Estate's self-declaration to the end of the Terror - the key turning points of the Revolution across five eventful years.

14 July 1789: the storming of the Bastille

As rumors spread of royal troop movements around Paris, the angry population feared a violent crackdown on the National Assembly. On 14 July 1789, a crowd stormed the Bastille, a fortress and state prison in eastern Paris, hoping to seize weapons and gunpowder believed to be stored there. Militarily, the Bastille was almost meaningless by then - at the time of the storming, it held only seven prisoners - but symbolically, it stood like no other building for the king's arbitrary power to imprison people without trial. The storming of the Bastille is still regarded today as the true beginning of the Revolution and is celebrated in France as its national holiday.

The night of 4 August and the Rights of Man

On the night of 4-5 August 1789, the National Assembly, in a remarkable session, abolished feudal rights and the privileges of the estates almost entirely. On 26 August 1789, it then adopted the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen (Déclaration des droits de l'homme et du citoyen). The text set out principles that still resonate in modern constitutions today: the freedom and equality of all people from birth, the right to property, security, and resistance against oppression, and popular sovereignty as the source of all state power. For its time, this was a radical break with the principle of divine right, under which the king received his power directly from God.

💬 Mia asks Grandpa Theo

Mia

Why did the French overthrow their king - hadn't he always been in charge?

Grandpa Theo

Because several problems escalated at once: the state was practically bankrupt, partly because of expensive wars, while the nobility and clergy paid almost no taxes. At the same time, bad harvests caused famine, and Enlightenment ideas led many people to question the king's divine right to rule in the first place. When the Third Estate declared itself the National Assembly in 1789, that was the spark that set everything in motion.

Mia

But then why did the revolutionaries themselves execute so many people with the guillotine, if it was all supposed to be about freedom?

Grandpa Theo

That's exactly the question historians still argue about. Robespierre himself justified the Terror as a necessary defense of the young republic against enemies at home and abroad - he spoke of “virtue and terror” as two sides of the same coin. Some historians see the Terror as an almost inevitable result of war and existential fear, while others stress that it also grew out of the radical revolutionary ideology itself. What's clear is this: the guillotine shows that the lofty ideals of 1789 and the violent practice of 1793-94 cannot simply be separated from each other - both belong to the story of the Revolution.

From monarchy to republic and the Terror

The Revolution did not stop with the 1791 constitution and constitutional monarchy, however. When King Louis XVI secretly tried to flee Paris in 1791 and was caught at Varennes, trust in him was permanently destroyed. War broke out against Austria and Prussia in 1792 and radicalized the situation further: in September 1792, the monarchy was abolished and the First Republic proclaimed, and in January 1793, Louis XVI was executed by guillotine for treason. Under the pressure of war, economic crisis, and internal opposition, the Committee of Public Safety, dominated by Maximilien Robespierre, effectively seized power and launched the so-called Reign of Terror (1793-94): under the “Law of Suspects”, thousands of alleged enemies of the state could be convicted without proper trial and publicly executed by guillotine - in total, depending on the estimate, between 17,000 and over 40,000 people lost their lives.

As the Committee of Public Safety increasingly turned against its own ranks, fear grew among the remaining deputies. On 27 July 1794 (“9 Thermidor” in the revolutionary calendar of the time), Robespierre himself was overthrown and executed the very next day - the Reign of Terror was over. A more moderate government, the Directory, followed (1795-99), but it remained politically unstable and was finally ended in 1799 by a coup led by the young general Napoleon Bonaparte. Napoleon took power first as “First Consul”, later as emperor (from 1804) - he preserved many revolutionary achievements, such as legal equality under the Napoleonic Code, the Code civil, but ruled once again in an authoritarian style. The Revolution thus entered a new phase, dominated by a single man.

Important for advanced students

The French Revolution is considered a break between historical eras because it replaced the principle of divine right with popular sovereignty, and because the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen (26 August 1789) became a lasting model still referenced today. Also important for exams: historians disagree on when the Revolution actually “ends” - some accounts place the end in 1794 (Robespierre's fall), others in 1799 (Napoleon's coup), and still others in 1804 (his coronation as emperor). A good answer names this ambiguity, rather than uncritically presenting a single date as “the end”.

Four confusions that cost marks

These four points are where most marks are lost in exams.

The Bastille held seven prisoners, not hundreds
Four forgers, two men classed as insane and one nobleman. There were no political prisoners to free. The crowd came for the gunpowder stored there — the importance of the day is symbolic, not practical.
Marie Antoinette never said the cake line
"Let them eat brioche" appears in essence in Rousseau's Confessions, written around 1765. Marie Antoinette only came to France in 1770 and was a child in Vienna at the time. The line was pinned on her later.
The Declaration of Rights did not apply to everyone
Women, enslaved people in the colonies and those without property were initially excluded. Olympe de Gouges published a Declaration of the Rights of Woman in 1791 and was executed in 1793. Naming that contradiction makes for a fuller answer than praise alone.
The Revolution did not end in 1789
The storming of the Bastille was its beginning, not its conclusion. A constitutional monarchy followed, then a republic, the execution of the king, the Terror and the Directory. The usual end point is Napoleon's coup in 1799.

Practice: five questions, instant feedback

Click an answer. You see straight away whether it is right — and why.

  1. How many prisoners were in the Bastille on 14 July 1789?

    Four forgers, two men classed as insane and one nobleman. The crowd came for the gunpowder stored inside. The importance of the day lies in the symbol: a royal fortress had fallen.

  2. Why did Louis XVI convene the Estates-General in 1789?

    It was a last resort in a financial crisis — the Estates-General had not met for 175 years. The tax question turned into a revolution because of the voting rules: the third estate represented most of the population but held only one vote in three.

  3. What did the National Assembly decide on the night of 4–5 August 1789?

    In a single night's session the legal basis of the society of estates fell. Three weeks later came the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen.

  4. Did the Declaration of Rights of 1789 apply to everyone?

    The claim was universal, the practice was not. Olympe de Gouges demanded the rights of woman in a declaration of her own in 1791 and was executed in 1793. That contradiction belongs in a good answer.

  5. When is the French Revolution usually said to have ended?

    1789 was the start. Constitutional monarchy, republic, Terror and Directory followed. The usual end point is Napoleon's coup of 1799.

Exam-style questions with model answers

Write your own answer first, then open the model one.

Explain the causes of the French Revolution.

Social: France was divided into three estates. Nobility and clergy enjoyed privileges and were largely exempt from tax, while the third estate — about 98 per cent of the population — carried the burden and had little political weight.

Economic: wars and the cost of the court had left the state effectively bankrupt. Failed harvests drove up the price of bread, which absorbed a large share of ordinary wages.

Intellectual: the Enlightenment had spread ideas such as popular sovereignty, the separation of powers and natural rights, providing a ready alternative to rule by divine right.

Political: convening the Estates-General was meant to solve the financial crisis but exposed the conflict over voting rules — the third estate demanded voting by head rather than by estate.

What matters is the convergence: none of these causes alone would have produced 1789.

Assess the significance of the Declaration of the Rights of Man of 26 August 1789.

Content: the Declaration set out liberty, property, security and resistance to oppression as natural rights, bound all authority to law, and made the nation the bearer of sovereignty.

Effect: it became a model for countless later constitutions and still echoes in the Universal Declaration of Human Rights of 1948. Its lasting core is that rights belong to people rather than being granted by a ruler.

Limits: women, the propertyless and enslaved people in the colonies were excluded. Slavery was abolished only in 1794 and reintroduced by Napoleon in 1802.

Weighing it up: the Declaration matters less as a description of reality than as a standard — it handed those who remained excluded an argument with which to demand their own equality.

Explain how the Declaration of Rights could be followed by the Terror.

After 1789 the Revolution came under pressure from two sides: several European monarchies went to war against France, while revolts and royalist resistance broke out at home.

In that situation the Convention gave the Committee of Public Safety sweeping powers. A state of emergency and a sense of threat became the justification for suspending the very legal safeguards that had been proclaimed in 1789.

The logic of threat then widened: first it struck declared enemies, then dissenters within the movement itself. When the Committee increasingly turned on its own allies, they struck back and overthrew Robespierre on 27 July 1794.

Historians still disagree whether the Terror followed mainly from the war emergency or was latent in the idea of a single valid truth. A good answer names both readings.
