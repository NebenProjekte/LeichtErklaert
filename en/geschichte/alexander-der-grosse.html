Alexander the Great Explained Simply

Written by Viktor Jonas · Last reviewed 25 October 2026 · Reading time about 10 minutes

Alexander the Great (356-323 BCE) was king of Macedonia and, in only about twelve years, conquered one of the largest empires of antiquity - stretching from Greece across Egypt and the entire Near East all the way to India. What makes him historically significant, though, is not just the sheer scale of his campaign: his conquests fused Greek and Near Eastern cultures into something entirely new, later called "Hellenism" - a cultural era that shaped the Mediterranean world for centuries.

What Alexander inherited from his father

The foundation for Alexander's success was laid by his father Philip II, king of Macedonia from 359 BCE. Philip united the previously feuding Greek city-states under Macedonian leadership after defeating them at the Battle of Chaeronea in 338 BCE, and he built a highly professional army - including the famous Macedonian phalanx (infantry wielding pikes up to six meters long, called sarissas) and a powerful cavalry. When Philip was assassinated in 336 BCE, the twenty-year-old Alexander inherited both the throne and the army - and with them, his father's plan to attack the mighty Persian Empire.

Only eleven years separate the start of the Persian campaign in 334 BCE from Alexander's death in 323 BCE - in that short span, the political map of the ancient world was fundamentally redrawn.

The Persian campaign: Issus, Egypt, Gaugamela

In 334 BCE, Alexander crossed into Asia Minor with his army and launched the campaign against the Persian Empire, ruled by Great King Darius III. After early victories, the decisive Battle of Issus took place in 333 BCE, where Alexander, despite being badly outnumbered, won a crucial victory and forced Darius to flee - even Darius's family fell into Alexander's hands in the process. Rather than attacking Persia directly, Alexander marched south, conquering the Levant and finally Egypt, where he was welcomed as a liberator from Persian rule. There, in 331 BCE, he founded the city of Alexandria, which would become one of the most important trade and scholarly centers of the ancient world - one of roughly twenty cities he founded during his campaign, many of which bore his name.

The war against Persia was finally decided in 331 BCE at the Battle of Gaugamela (in modern-day Iraq), where Alexander won through superior tactics despite being massively outnumbered - Persian sources describe a force many times the size of his own. Darius III fled again and was murdered shortly afterward by his own men; Alexander had him buried with royal honors and from then on styled himself as the rightful successor to the Persian Great Kings. The Persian capitals of Babylon, Susa, and Persepolis fell into his hands - and the destruction of Persepolis's magnificent palace by fire remains disputed to this day: was it a deliberate act of revenge for the Persian destruction of Athens a century and a half earlier, or an accident during a night of heavy drinking? Historians still disagree.

As far as India — and the limit of his own army

After defeating Persia, Alexander pressed on eastward, through what is now Afghanistan and Uzbekistan, all the way into India. In 326 BCE he defeated the Indian king Porus, who fielded war elephants, at the Battle of the Hydaspes. But at this point, Alexander's own soldiers refused to go any further: after years of unbroken campaigning far from home, the exhausted troops mutinied at the Hyphasis River and forced Alexander to turn back. On the retreat through the Gedrosian Desert, thousands of soldiers died of heat and thirst - a reminder that Alexander's glory came at a steep human cost, paid not only by the peoples he conquered but also by his own men.

💬 Mia asks Grandpa Theo

Mia

Was Alexander really as heroic as his nickname "the Great" sounds?

Grandpa Theo

That depends on your perspective. He was an exceptional military talent, yes - but his campaign meant siege, destruction, and death for many cities. Thebes was razed to the ground in 335 BCE, and Tyre was captured in 332 BCE after a months-long siege, with much of its population killed or enslaved. "Great" doesn't automatically mean "good" - it describes his historical impact, not a moral judgment.

Mia

So what happened to his huge empire after he died?

Grandpa Theo

That's exactly where it gets interesting: Alexander died in 323 BCE in Babylon, probably of a fever - historians still debate whether malaria, typhoid fever, or even poisoning was behind it. He had named no successor and is said to have declared on his deathbed only that the empire should go "to the strongest." What followed was almost inevitable: his generals, the "Diadochi," fought each other for decades in the Wars of the Diadochi, until the empire split into several separate kingdoms.

Death in Babylon and the break-up of the empire

The decades-long Wars of the Diadochi ultimately produced several major Hellenistic kingdoms: the Ptolemaic kingdom in Egypt (home to the famous Library of Alexandria, a center of ancient scholarship), the Seleucid kingdom, which stretched across much of western Asia, and Macedonia itself under new ruling dynasties. More important than this political division, though, is the cultural consequence of the era: Hellenism. Greek language (in the form of "Koine," a simplified common dialect), Greek architecture, art, and science fused with Egyptian, Persian, and Mesopotamian traditions into a new, cosmopolitan culture. Greek became the common language of the educated Mediterranean world for centuries - a fact that later even helped the spread of Christianity, whose earliest texts were written in Greek. Alexandria produced major scholarly works, including those of the mathematician Euclid and the scholar Eratosthenes, who calculated the Earth's circumference with remarkable accuracy.

Key for advanced exams

Three key dates for exams: 333 BCE (Issus), 331 BCE (Gaugamela), and 323 BCE (Alexander's death in Babylon). Also remember: the term "Hellenism" does not refer to Alexander's campaign itself, but to the cultural fusion of Greek and Near Eastern elements that fully developed in the successor states only after his death - historians often judge this cultural legacy to be more significant than the military conquests themselves.

Four confusions that cost marks

These are the points where most marks are lost in exams.

Issus is not Gaugamela
At Issus in 333 BCE Alexander beat Darius III and forced him to flee — but the war was not decided by it. The decision came only in 331 BCE at Gaugamela in present-day Iraq.
Philip II is not a footnote
The army, the phalanx with its sarissas and the dominance over Greece after the victory at Chaeronea in 338 BCE were all built by the father. In 336 BCE Alexander inherited a finished instrument — leave that out and you explain only half the success.
The campaign did not end in India because Alexander wanted it to
He still won at the Hydaspes in 326 BCE, but the exhausted army refused to march on. Turning back was forced, not planned.
Hellenism is not the same as “ancient Greece”
The term means the era after Alexander, in which Greek and Near Eastern cultures mixed — not the classical Greece that came before it.

Practice: five questions, instant feedback

Click an answer. You will see at once whether it is right — and why.

  1. How old was Alexander when he became king?

    Twenty. His father Philip II was assassinated in 336 BCE, and Alexander was born in 356 BCE.

  2. Which battle finally decided the war against the Persian Empire?

    Gaugamela. At Issus Darius III did flee, but he was able to raise a new army. Chaeronea was still the father's victory over the Greek city-states.

  3. Why did Alexander turn back from India in 326 BCE?

    The army was worn out after years in the field and refused to go further. He had won the Battle of the Hydaspes, and Darius had been dead for years by then.

  4. What does the term Hellenism refer to?

    Hellenism begins with Alexander's death in 323 BCE. Classical Athens comes before it, Roman rule follows later.

  5. What happened to the empire after Alexander's death?

    The Diadochi, his generals, fought over the succession for decades. Several Hellenistic kingdoms grew out of it, among them the Ptolemaic kingdom in Egypt.

Exam-style questions with model answers

Try it yourself first, then open the answer.

Explain why Alexander's success cannot be understood without Philip II.

Philip II created three preconditions: a standing, drilled professional army with the sarissa phalanx and a powerful cavalry; political dominance over Greece after the victory at Chaeronea in 338 BCE, which kept Alexander's back free; and a finished plan for a Persian campaign, alliance structure included.

So in 336 BCE Alexander inherited not just a throne but a ready instrument. His own contribution lies in leadership in the field and in speed — not in building the means.

Assess the epithet “the Great”.

In favour: the military and organisational achievement. In eleven years, an empire from Greece to the Indus, roughly twenty city foundations, and a cultural effect that lasted well into Roman times.

Against: the price. Campaigns with tens of thousands dead, the destruction of Thebes in 335 BCE and the burning of Persepolis, plus an empire that outlived its founder by less than two years because no succession had been arranged.

A defensible judgement separates the standards: by ancient standards of glory and conquest he was exceptional; by today's standards the epithet is a valuation, not a description. That distinction is exactly what earns the marks.

Name the three dates you have to know, and place them.

333 BCE, Issus: the first great victory over Darius III, the Great King flees, the road south is open.

331 BCE, Gaugamela: the decision, and the collapse of the Persian Empire. Alexandria was founded in Egypt the same year.

323 BCE, Babylon: Alexander's death at 32, the start of the Wars of the Diadochi and at the same time of the Hellenistic era.
