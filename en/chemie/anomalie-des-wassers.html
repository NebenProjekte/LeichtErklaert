The Anomaly of Water Explained Simply

For almost every substance, the solid form is denser than the liquid form - a solid lump sinks in its own melt. For water, it is exactly the other way round: ice is less dense than liquid water, which is why it floats on top. This is called the density anomaly of water.

Density(ice) < Density(water at 4°C) liquid water reaches its highest density at about 4°C - both cooling it into ice and heating it further make it less dense

Because ice is lighter than water, a layer of ice forms on top of a lake first, while the water underneath stays liquid.

💬 Mia asks Grandpa Theo

Mia

Why does water actually get lighter when it freezes, instead of heavier like other substances?

Grandpa Theo

It comes down to the special shape of water molecules. When water freezes, the molecules arrange themselves into a crystal lattice in which each molecule sits a bit farther from its neighbours than in liquid water. That means ice needs more space - so for the same mass, it has a lower density.

Mia

Why is 4°C, of all temperatures, the one with the highest density?

Grandpa Theo

At 4°C two opposing effects are in balance: warmer water expands normally and becomes lighter as a result. Colder water, however, already starts to arrange itself towards that looser ice structure, which also makes it lighter. At 4°C the water is packed most densely - colder and warmer both make it lighter again.

Mia

Wouldn't it actually be all the same whether ice floats on top or at the bottom?

Grandpa Theo

Not at all - it is even vital for survival! Because ice floats on top, a lake freezes from the top down. Underneath, the water stays liquid, so fish and other aquatic life can survive the winter. If ice sank, many lakes and ponds would eventually freeze solid all the way to the bottom.

Try it yourself

Put an ice cube into a glass of water - it floats on top, but a large part of it dips below the surface. This very proportion shows how much lighter ice really is compared with water.

The density anomaly of water is one of the reasons why life in lakes and oceans on Earth is possible even in cold winters.

Key terms

Density anomaly — water has its greatest density at 4 °C, not at 0 °C.
Hydrogen bonds — weak bonds between water molecules; they hold the crystal lattice at a distance.
Dipole — the water molecule is bent and therefore electrically asymmetric.
Thermocline — the temperature boundary in a lake between warm surface water and cold deep water.

Why ice is lighter than water

When water freezes, the molecules arrange themselves through hydrogen bonds into a hexagonal lattice. This lattice is looser than the arrangement in liquid water — the volume increases by around nine percent, and the density falls to 0.92 g/cm³. This is why ice floats, and why water pipes burst in frost.

What this means for lakes

In winter, cooled surface water sinks — but only down to 4 °C. Even colder water is lighter and stays on top. This is why a lake freezes from the top, and at the bottom a steady temperature of about 4 °C remains. Fish and aquatic plants survive the winter there. If ice sank, lakes would freeze completely through from the bottom up.

Typical mistakes

"Water is densest at 0 °C." It is densest at 4 °C.
Confusing the anomaly with the high boiling point. Both go back to hydrogen bonds, but they are not the same thing.
Reading expansion on freezing as the rule. Almost all other substances contract when they solidify — water is the exception.
Misjudging icebergs. Around 90% lies below the water, because the density difference is only 8%.

Practice question

A full glass bottle of water (0.7 l) is forgotten in the freezer. Explain what happens, and calculate the volume of the ice that forms.

Show solution

Mass: 0.7 l of water = 700 g.
Volume as ice: V = m ÷ ρ = 700 ÷ 0.917 = 763 cm³, so a good 0.76 l.

About 63 cm³ more space is needed than the bottle provides. Since water is practically incompressible, the growing ice lattice pushes outward with very great force — the bottle bursts. The same happens with water pipes and, in nature, with cracks in rock: frost wedging is one of the most effective forces of weathering.
