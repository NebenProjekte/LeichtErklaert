Redox Reactions Explained Simply

Written by Viktor Jonas · Last checked on 24 October 2026 · Reading time about 10 minutes

A nail rusts over months, a match burns out in seconds, and a battery delivers current for weeks. Three completely different processes - and chemically the same principle. In all three, electrons travel from one particle to another. Reactions like this are called redox reactions, and the name is simply short for the two halves they always consist of: reduction and oxidation.

Oxidation = giving electrons away · Reduction = taking electrons on Both always happen at the same time - wherever something is given away, something else must take it

Each half-equation shows only one half of what happens. Add them together and the electrons cancel out, leaving the overall equation - which is exactly how you can tell that the number of electrons given away must match the number taken on.

Where the name oxidation comes from

The term oxidation originally came from oxygen. For a long time it meant any reaction in which a substance combines with oxygen: iron rusts, magnesium burns, wood turns to ash. Once chemists understood what was happening at particle level, it turned out that oxygen is not required at all. What matters is the transfer of electrons. That is why the wider definition applies today - and the old one is contained within it as a special case, because oxygen is simply an unusually greedy electron acceptor.

Oxidising and reducing agents

Two roles matter in practice. The substance that gives electrons away and is itself oxidised is called the reducing agent - after all, it makes the reduction of the other one possible. The substance that takes electrons on and is itself reduced is called the oxidising agent. The naming looks back-to-front at first, but it is consistent: it describes not what happens to the substance itself, but what it does to its partner.

Oxidation numbers

To spot the electron transfer even where no ions are formed, chemists use oxidation numbers. You pretend that every bond is ionic and assign the bonding electrons to whichever partner has the higher electronegativity. A handful of rules is enough for school: elements in their pure form have oxidation number 0. For single-atom ions it equals the charge. Oxygen is almost always −2, hydrogen usually +1. And the sum of all oxidation numbers in a neutral molecule is 0. If the oxidation number goes up during the reaction, that species was oxidised; if it goes down, it was reduced.

The order is not arbitrary but measured. Hydrogen serves as the zero point: metals to its left dissolve in acid while giving off hydrogen, metals to its right do not.

The reactivity series

Which of two metals gives its electrons away is shown by the reactivity series. On the left are the reactive metals such as potassium, sodium and magnesium - they release their outer electrons readily. On the right are the noble ones like silver and gold, which hold on to them. When a more reactive metal meets the ions of a nobler one, the reaction proceeds: an iron nail in copper sulfate solution gets coated in a layer of copper, because iron is more reactive than copper. Put copper into iron sulfate solution and nothing happens. This same ordering also explains why gold occurs in nature as a metal while iron is only found as ore - gold simply will not part with its electrons.

💬 Mia asks Grandpa Theo

Mia

I keep mixing up oxidation and reduction. Is there a trick?

Grandpa Theo

Use the oxidation number as your anchor: oxidation means it goes up, reduction means it comes down. And if you are wondering where the electrons go: in a half-equation they always sit on the side that wants to be rid of them. On the right for oxidation, on the left for reduction.

Mia

Why does iron rust but gold does not?

Grandpa Theo

Because iron is reactive and gold is noble. Iron readily hands its electrons to the oxygen in the air, and with water in the mix you get rust. Gold holds on to its electrons, so air and water do nothing to it. That is why you find nuggets of gold in rivers but never nuggets of iron - the iron would long since have turned into ore.

Mia

And why does zinc protect the iron in galvanised nails, when zinc corrodes too?

Grandpa Theo

That is exactly why it works. Zinc is more reactive than iron, so it gives its electrons away first. As long as there is zinc left, it is sacrificed and the iron is spared - that is called a sacrificial anode. On top of that, zinc forms a dense oxide layer that stays put, while rust flakes off and keeps exposing fresh iron.

A battery is a redox reaction on a leash

Put a zinc rod into copper sulfate solution and the reaction runs immediately - the electrons jump straight across and the energy is lost as heat. The key idea behind a battery is to forbid the two halves from touching. In the Daniell cell, a zinc rod sits in zinc sulfate solution and a copper rod in copper sulfate solution, separated into two containers. The electrons can now only go the long way round, through a wire - and that forced detour is the electric current you can put to use. To keep the circuit closed, a salt bridge connects the two solutions; it lets ions migrate and stops either side from building up an electrical charge. Metal dissolves at the zinc, fresh copper grows on the copper rod, and when the zinc is used up the battery is flat. A rechargeable cell is the same arrangement, just with reactions that can be driven backwards by applying an external voltage.

Related topics

Why atoms want to give electrons away or take them on in the first place is covered in atomic structure and the periodic table. What becomes of the ions that form is explained by chemical bonding. And the fact that your own body runs on electron transfers is shown in cellular respiration.

The sentence to remember for the test: whoever gives electrons away is oxidised, whoever takes them on is reduced. And you never get one without the other - which is why it is called a redox reaction and not just an ox reaction.

Practice: five questions, instant feedback

Click an answer. You see straight away whether it is right — and why.

  1. What happens during an oxidation?

    Giving electrons away is the core of it. Oxygen was the historical reason for the name, but it is not required. Sodium reacting with chlorine is an oxidation too.

  2. A substance takes electrons on and is thereby reduced. What is its role called?

    The name describes what the substance does to its partner, not what happens to itself. Taking electrons on oxidises the other substance — hence oxidising agent. The reducing agent works the other way round.

  3. Can oxidation and reduction happen separately?

    Electrons do not vanish. Every donation needs a receiver, which is why the overall process is called a redox reaction. A battery does separate the two halves in space, but even there the electrons travel to the partner through the wire.

  4. Why does iron rust while gold does not?

    Iron sits far to the left in the reactivity series, gold at the far right. Iron hands its electrons to the oxygen in the air; gold keeps them. That is why gold coins still come out of the ground shiny after centuries.

  5. Why does a zinc coating protect an iron nail even though zinc itself reacts?

    That is the trick behind galvanising: the zinc sacrifices itself. As long as any zinc is left it is oxidised in place of the iron — even where the coating is already scratched. A coat of paint, by contrast, only protects while it stays intact.

Exam-style questions with model answers

Write your own answer first, then open the model one.

Set up the half equations for zinc reacting with copper sulfate solution and name the roles.

Oxidation: Zn → Zn2+ + 2 e−. The zinc gives two electrons away and goes into solution as an ion.

Reduction: Cu2+ + 2 e− → Cu. The copper ions take the electrons on and deposit as metal.

Overall: Zn + Cu2+ → Zn2+ + Cu.

Roles: zinc is the reducing agent, because it makes the reduction of the copper possible. The copper ions are the oxidising agent.

What you observe: the zinc bar grows thinner, red-brown copper deposits on it, and the blue colour of the solution fades.

Explain why the terms oxidising agent and reducing agent seem back to front at first sight.

The confusion comes from applying the names to the substance itself. In fact they describe its effect on the partner.

A reducing agent gives electrons away. It is itself oxidised in the process — but it triggers the reduction of the partner, and that is what it is named after.

An oxidising agent takes electrons on, is itself reduced, and oxidises the partner.

A memory hook for the exam: the agent is always named after what it does to the other substance. Once that clicks, the confusion does not come back.

Describe the Daniell cell and explain why the two halves have to be separated.

A zinc bar stands in zinc sulfate solution and a copper bar in copper sulfate solution. The two vessels are joined by a salt bridge and the bars by a wire.

Oxidation happens at the zinc, reduction at the copper. Because the halves are separate, the electrons cannot jump across directly: they have to take the detour through the wire — and that forced detour is the electric current.

Put the zinc bar straight into the copper solution and the same reaction runs, but the electrons transfer immediately. The energy is released as heat and cannot be used.

The salt bridge balances the charges. Without it one side would charge up positively and the other negatively, and the current would stop within moments. The voltage is 1.10 volts.
