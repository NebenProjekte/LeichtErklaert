The Three States of Matter (Solid, Liquid, Gas) Explained Simply

Written by Viktor Jonas · Last checked on 24 October 2026 · Reading time about 10 minutes

Almost every substance can exist in three different forms: solid, liquid, or gas. Which form it takes depends mostly on temperature - and the exact temperature where it switches is completely different from substance to substance. Water already turns solid at 0°C, while iron doesn't turn solid until you drop below over 1500°C. Let's take a close look at why.

solid ⇆ liquid ⇆ gas melting/freezing and boiling/condensing - the change works in both directions

The more energy (heat) the particles get, the more strongly they move - from the ordered lattice, through the mobile liquid, to the freely flying gas.

The three states at particle level

In the solid state, particles (atoms, ions, or molecules) sit fixed in place in an ordered lattice. They can't travel through the substance, only vibrate on the spot - which is why a solid object keeps its shape and volume on its own.

In the liquid state, particles are packed almost as tightly as in the solid state, but they can slide past one another. That's why a liquid takes the shape of its container while keeping its volume - you can't noticeably compress water.

In the gas state, particles barely touch each other at all. They fly through space quickly and randomly, spreading out evenly until they hit a wall. That's why a gas always fills the entire available space and can easily be compressed.

Melting and boiling points

The switch between states doesn't happen at just any temperature - it happens at a point that's exactly fixed for each substance: the melting point (solid → liquid) and the boiling point (liquid → gas), both measured at standard pressure (1013 hPa, roughly the air pressure at sea level). 💬 Mia asks Grandpa Theo

Mia

Why does water already turn solid at 0°C, but iron not until above 1500°C?

Grandpa Theo

It depends on how strongly the particles are held together. In metals like iron, very strong metallic bonds hold the atoms together - breaking those takes an enormous amount of energy, meaning high temperature. Water molecules, on the other hand, are only held together by comparatively weak hydrogen bonds, which break apart with much less energy.

Mia

Why are gold's and silver's melting and boiling points so close together, but still not exactly the same?

Grandpa Theo

Gold and silver atoms are chemically very similar - both are noble metals with similarly strong metallic bonds. But gold atoms are a bit heavier and their bonds are minimally stronger, so gold's melting point - and especially its boiling point - sits a little higher than silver's.

Mia

Does water actually boil at exactly 100°C everywhere in the world?

Grandpa Theo

Only at standard pressure at sea level! The lower the air pressure, the more easily water particles can escape - so water boils at a lower temperature high up a mountain. On a 5,000-meter peak, for example, water already boils at around 83°C.

Mia

What about glass? When does that turn solid or liquid?

Grandpa Theo

That's exactly what makes glass complicated - unlike water, iron, gold, and silver, glass doesn't have a fixed melting point at all. Let me explain that in more detail.

The Special Case of Glass

Water, iron, gold, and silver are crystalline substances - their particles arrange into an exact, repeating lattice in the solid state, which is exactly why there's a precise point where that lattice breaks down. Glass, on the other hand, is amorphous: even in its solid state, its particles stay in a disordered arrangement, almost like a frozen liquid. That's why there's no sharp melting point, only a gradual transition range (called the glass transition range) in which glass just keeps getting softer. For ordinary window glass, this range starts at around 550-600°C, and it only becomes properly flowable and workable at around 1400-1600°C. This is exactly the soft transition that glassblowers rely on to shape glass, since it never suddenly flips from solid to fully liquid.

Whether a substance is solid, liquid, or gas at room temperature isn't random - it comes down to how strongly its particles are held together, and that's exactly what makes water, metals, and glass behave so differently in everyday life.

Why boiling water stays at 100 °C

Put a pot of water on the hob and the temperature climbs steadily until it reaches 100 °C — and then it stops, no matter how high you turn the heat. The energy going in does not disappear; it is used entirely to break the bonds between the molecules so they can leave the liquid. Physicists call this the latent heat of vaporisation, and for water it is unusually large: 2257 kilojoules per kilogram, roughly seven times what it takes to heat that same kilogram from room temperature to boiling point. The same effect makes sweating such an efficient way to cool down. Every gram of water that evaporates from the skin takes about 2.4 kilojoules with it, which is why a breeze on damp skin feels so much colder than the air temperature alone would suggest.

Practice: five questions, instant feedback

Click an answer. You see straight away whether it is right — and why.

  1. What is different about the particles in a solid compared with a liquid?

    The particles themselves do not change at all — only how they move. In a solid they vibrate about fixed positions; in a liquid they are just as close together but can slide past one another, which is why a liquid takes the shape of its container.

  2. Why does iron melt at a much higher temperature than water?

    Melting means overcoming the forces that hold particles in place. In a metal the electron sea binds the cores very strongly, so it takes 1538 °C. Water molecules are held by much weaker hydrogen bonds and let go at 0 °C.

  3. Water is boiling in a pot. What happens to the temperature while it boils?

    All the added energy goes into separating the molecules rather than into speeding them up. This is the heat of vaporisation. The temperature only starts climbing again once there is no liquid left.

  4. Does water always boil at exactly 100 °C?

    The lower the air pressure, the more easily molecules escape the liquid. On a high mountain water can boil at around 70 °C, which is why food takes longer to cook up there.

  5. Why is glass a special case?

    Crystalline substances have an ordered lattice that breaks down at one sharp temperature. Glass keeps its disordered arrangement even when solid, so it has a softening range rather than a melting point. The old story that old window panes flow downwards over centuries is, however, a myth.

Exam-style questions with model answers

Work it out yourself first, then open the answer.

Describe the three states at particle level and explain how shape and volume follow from them.

Solid: particles sit close together in fixed positions and only vibrate. The substance therefore keeps both its shape and its volume.

Liquid: particles are almost as close together but can slide past one another. The volume stays fixed, the shape adapts to the container.

Gas: particles are far apart and move freely at high speed. Neither shape nor volume is fixed; a gas fills whatever space it is given.

The decisive point: the particles themselves are identical in all three states. What changes is only how strongly they are held and how freely they move.

Explain why the temperature of boiling water stays constant even though heat is still being supplied.

Temperature is a measure of the average kinetic energy of the particles. While the water heats up, the supplied energy does indeed increase that energy and the temperature rises.

At the boiling point the situation changes. The energy now goes into separating molecules from one another against their mutual attraction — the heat of vaporisation. It is stored as potential energy, not as faster motion, so the thermometer does not move.

Only when the last of the liquid has evaporated does further energy raise the temperature of the steam again. The same applies at the melting point with the heat of fusion, which is why an ice-water mixture stays at 0 °C until the last piece of ice is gone.

Nitrogen melts at −210 °C and boils at −196 °C. What state is it in at room temperature, and what does that tell you about the forces between its particles?

Room temperature of about 20 °C lies far above the boiling point of −196 °C, so nitrogen is a gas.

That means the forces between nitrogen molecules are extremely weak: even at 77 kelvin above absolute zero they can no longer hold the molecules together.

The comparison is instructive. Water, with its strong hydrogen bonds, is still liquid at 20 °C; iron, with its metallic bonding, is solid up to 1538 °C. The state at room temperature is therefore a direct read-out of bond strength — which is why the same question is often asked the other way round in exams.
