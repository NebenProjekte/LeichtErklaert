Radioactivity Explained Simply

Radioactivity means: an atomic nucleus is unstable and transforms itself. In doing so it emits radiation. This happens by itself, without a trigger — you can neither switch it on nor switch it off.

N(t) = N₀ · (1/2)t / T N₀ initial amount · T half-life · after each half-life, half remains
Key terms

Alpha radiation — helium nuclei. Strongly ionising, but stopped even by paper.
Beta radiation — electrons or positrons. Stopped by a few millimetres of aluminium.
Gamma radiation — high-energy electromagnetic waves. Needs lead or concrete.
Half-life — the time after which half of the nuclei have decayed.
Becquerel (Bq) — decays per second, the activity.
Sievert (Sv) — the biological effect on the body.

The apparent contradiction with alpha resolves like this: from outside, the radiation does not get through the skin. But if the material is inhaled or swallowed, there is no protective layer any more — and then alpha in particular is especially harmful, because it releases its energy over a short distance.

💬 Mia asks Grandpa Theo

Mia

Does it ever become completely zero?

Grandpa Theo

Mathematically never — there is always half of the half left. In practice, after ten half-lives less than a thousandth remains.

Mia

And why is a short half-life more dangerous?

Grandpa Theo

Because in the same time many more nuclei decay. Something with a half-life of millions of years hardly radiates — it is in no hurry.

Typical mistakes

"After two half-lives everything is gone." One quarter is left, not zero.
Thinking a long half-life is more dangerous. The other way round: a short half-life means high activity.
Mixing up becquerel and sievert. Bq counts decays, Sv describes the effect in tissue.
Thinking irradiated food is radioactive. Irradiation kills germs but leaves no radiation behind.

Practice question

A sample of caesium-137 (half-life 30 years) has 640 Bq today. What was the activity 90 years ago, and what will it be in 60 years?

Show solution

90 years are three half-lives backwards, so double three times:
640 · 2³ = 5,120 Bq

60 years are two half-lives forwards:
640 ÷ 2² = 160 Bq

This is exactly why caesium-137 is so problematic after reactor accidents: 30 years is long enough for it to stay in the soil for decades, and short enough for it to radiate strongly in the meantime.

How radiation is measured

In radioactivity, three units are easily mixed up, and they actually measure three completely different things.

The becquerel (Bq) measures the activity of a sample: one becquerel means one nuclear decay per second. That says something about the material, but nothing about what the radiation does. The gray (Gy) measures the absorbed dose, that is, how much energy actually stays in one kilogram of tissue. And the sievert (Sv) measures the effect on the body. It additionally takes into account that alpha radiation inside the body does around twenty times more damage than the same amount of energy from beta or gamma radiation. For everything to do with health, the sievert is therefore the right unit.

Orders of magnitude for perspective

In Germany, every person receives about 2 to 3 millisieverts per year from natural sources - from the ground, from building materials, from food and from space. A flight from Frankfurt to New York adds roughly 0.05 millisieverts, because at cruising altitude there is less atmosphere above you. A chest X-ray is about 0.02 millisieverts, a computed tomography scan of the abdomen, depending on the device, 5 to 20 millisieverts - that is, several times the natural annual dose. For people who are occupationally exposed to radiation, an upper limit of 20 millisieverts per year applies in Germany. These figures help with the most important perspective of all: radiation is not something that exists only in nuclear power plants. It is there all the time, and the decisive question is always how much.

You can protect yourself in three ways, and all three follow from what is said above about the types of radiation: distance, because the intensity falls with the square of the distance - double the distance means a quarter of the radiation. Shielding, where paper is enough for alpha, aluminium for beta, and for gamma thick layers of lead or concrete are needed. And time, because the dose absorbed simply grows with the length of the stay. Measurement is usually done with a Geiger-Müller counter tube, which makes every single decay audible as a click.

Natural radiation is everywhere — from the ground, building materials, food and space. In Germany the average exposure is about 2.1 millisieverts per year, and in some Alpine valleys twice that.
