Schrödinger's Cat Explained Simply

Written by Viktor Jonas · Last checked on 22 October 2026 · Reading time about 9 minutes

Picture this: a cat sits in a closed box. Inside there is also a radioactive atom, a detector and a vial of poison. If the atom decays, the detector triggers the mechanism, the poison is released and the cat dies. If it does not decay, the cat lives on. Nobody can see inside, and that is the whole point of the set-up.

|Cat⟩ = 1/√2 · ( |alive⟩ + |dead⟩ ) As long as nobody looks, quantum mechanics describes the atom — and therefore the cat too — as a superposition of both states at once

As long as the box stays closed, quantum mechanics says both possibilities are simultaneously "true" — only looking inside decides which reality occurs.

💬 Mia asks Grandpa Theo

Mia

Wait — is the cat really alive AND dead at the same time until you look inside?

Grandpa Theo

That is exactly the point. Schrödinger came up with this in 1935 not because he believed it, but to show how absurd it sounds when you apply the rules of the quantum world directly to an everyday object like a cat.

Mia

But where does the idea of superposition even come from?

Grandpa Theo

Very small particles like atoms and electrons really do behave that way — this is not nonsense. Before you measure, several possibilities exist at once as probabilities. Only the measurement "decides" on an outcome. For tiny particles this is well established. In everyday life, with big things like cats, it practically never happens.

Mia

Why doesn't it happen with the cat then — she's made of atoms too?

Grandpa Theo

Because a cat is made of countless billions of particles that are constantly interacting with their surroundings — air molecules, light, heat. Each of those interactions acts almost like a tiny measurement. The superposition collapses within a fraction of a second, long before anyone opens the box. Physicists call this decoherence.

Not just theory

Superposition is not merely a thought exercise. Modern quantum computers use exactly this principle. Unlike an ordinary bit, which is either 0 or 1, a "qubit" can hold both states at once for as long as it is not read out.

Key terms

Superposition — a quantum system is in several states at once as long as it is not measured.
Measurement — the interaction that pins the system down to one state.
Decoherence — the loss of superposition through contact with the surroundings.
Thought experiment — a set-up that is only ever played out in the mind.

What Schrödinger actually meant

The experiment is not a claim, it is a criticism. In 1935 Schrödinger found the then-standard interpretation of quantum mechanics unsatisfying, and he used the cat to show how absurd it becomes when you carry it over to large objects. A cat that is alive and dead at the same time was meant as an accusation, not as a description of reality. The irony is that the image became the most famous picture of quantum physics precisely because it was supposed to be ridiculous.

This is where the modern answer lies: decoherence. A large object is permanently interacting with its surroundings — through air molecules, thermal radiation, light. Every one of those interactions works like a measurement. The superposition falls apart so quickly that it never becomes observable. No human observer is required at any point, which removes the mystical flavour the story often gets.

Practice question

Use the idea of decoherence to explain why superposition can be measured for atoms in a laboratory but not for a cat.

Show solution

A single atom can be almost completely isolated from its surroundings: in a vacuum, at very low temperatures and shielded from light. Hardly any interactions take place, so the superposition survives long enough to be demonstrated through interference patterns. This has been achieved with molecules made of several hundred atoms.

A cat consists of roughly 1026 particles and constantly exchanges heat, air molecules and photons with its surroundings. Each of those processes carries information about its state to the outside world and acts like a measurement. The superposition therefore collapses in an unimaginably short time — on the order of 10−30 seconds and below. The transition from quantum behaviour to everyday behaviour is not a matter of principle, then, but a matter of isolation.

Three ways physicists answer the question

"Is the cat alive or dead before you look?" has no single agreed answer. The mathematics is undisputed and makes identical predictions in every case; what differs is what the formalism is taken to mean. The three positions below are the ones a school or first-year course will mention.

InterpretationWhat it says about the catWhat it leaves open
Copenhagen Before measurement only the probabilities are real. Measuring makes the state collapse to one outcome. What exactly counts as a measurement, and why collapse happens at all.
Many-worlds Nothing collapses. Both outcomes occur, in branches that no longer interact; you only ever see one of them. Why the branch you find yourself in feels singled out, and how probabilities arise.
Decoherence Contact with the surroundings destroys the superposition within fractions of a second — far too fast for anything cat-sized to stay in one. It explains why we never see superpositions, not which single outcome becomes real.

Decoherence is the part that is experimentally established: the bigger and warmer a system, the faster its superposition is destroyed. That is why quantum computers run close to absolute zero — and why a real cat would never be in a superposition in the first place.

Four mix-ups worth avoiding

Almost every popular account gets at least one of these wrong.

Schrödinger was not making a claim about cats
He proposed the set-up in 1935 as a criticism. His point was that applying the quantum rules unchanged to everyday objects produces an absurd picture — which shows something is missing from the account, not that cats live in superpositions.
"Observer" does not mean a conscious human
Any interaction that records information — a detector, a stray air molecule, a photon — does the job. Consciousness plays no role in the physics; that idea was added later by popularisers.
Superposition is not "we just don't know yet"
A coin under a cup already has a definite side up. A superposition is different: before measurement there is no hidden fact of the matter, and interference experiments prove it, because the possibilities can cancel each other out.
The cat was never really in a superposition
Decoherence makes sure of that. The thought experiment works on paper precisely because it ignores it — which is why it is a thought experiment and not a laboratory protocol.
Carrying quantum effects into everyday life
Superposition is something you can demonstrate for atoms and small molecules under laboratory conditions. Your keys are not in a superposition of places until you look for them — you simply do not remember where you put them.
„Physicists do not know what is going on“
The calculations of quantum mechanics are undisputed and have been confirmed millions of times over. What is argued about is the interpretation — what the maths says about reality — not the predictions themselves.

Practice: five questions, instant feedback

Click an answer. You see straight away whether it is right — and why.

  1. Why did Schrödinger invent this thought experiment in 1935?

    It was a criticism. Schrödinger wanted to make clear that taking the rules literally at human scale leads somewhere nobody is willing to go.

  2. What counts as an "observer" in quantum mechanics?

    A detector, a photon, even a single air molecule is enough. Consciousness is not part of the physics.

  3. How does a superposition differ from ordinary ignorance?

    A coin under a cup has a definite side already. A quantum system does not: the possibilities can interfere and cancel, which is impossible if one of them were secretly the case all along.

  4. What does decoherence explain?

    Contact with the environment destroys superpositions extremely fast — the larger and warmer the system, the faster. It does not say which single outcome you end up seeing.

  5. What distinguishes the many-worlds interpretation from the Copenhagen one?

    The predictions are identical; that is what makes the choice an interpretive one. Many-worlds drops the collapse and keeps both outcomes, each in its own branch.

Exam-style questions with model answers

Try them yourself first, then open the answer.

Explain the set-up of Schrödinger's thought experiment and say precisely what it is meant to show.

A cat sits in a sealed box with a radioactive atom, a detector and a vial of poison. Within an hour the atom has a fifty per cent chance of decaying; if it does, the detector releases the poison and the cat dies. The atom's state is genuinely a superposition of "decayed" and "not decayed".

The construction deliberately links a microscopic superposition to a macroscopic consequence. If the quantum description holds without restriction, the whole system — cat included — is in a superposition of "alive" and "dead" until it is observed.

The point is the contradiction: nobody has ever seen such a cat. So either the rules do not apply unchanged at that scale, or the word "measurement" is doing work the theory never defines. Schrödinger was exposing that gap, not describing cats.

Compare how the Copenhagen and many-worlds interpretations answer the question, and explain why the choice cannot be settled by experiment in the usual way.

Copenhagen: before measurement only probabilities are real; measurement collapses the state to a single outcome. The cat becomes alive or dead at the moment of observation.

Many-worlds: there is no collapse. The superposition continues and the observer becomes part of it, so both outcomes occur in branches that can no longer influence one another. Each version of the observer sees one result.

Both reproduce exactly the same measurement statistics, which is why no ordinary experiment separates them: they differ in what they say about what is not observed. A complete answer notes that this is a question about interpretation, and that physicists disagree without disagreeing about any prediction.

What role does decoherence play, and why does it not fully resolve the puzzle?

Decoherence describes what happens when a quantum system interacts with its surroundings. Information about its state leaks into the environment, and the interference between the possibilities disappears. The time this takes falls dramatically with size and temperature — for an object the size of a cat it is far shorter than any measurable interval.

That explains the appearance of our world: superpositions of large objects are never observed because they do not survive long enough.

What it does not explain is why exactly one outcome is found. Decoherence turns a superposition into something that looks like a list of classical alternatives, but the step from "list of possibilities" to "this one happened" still needs an interpretation. That is why the debate continues.
