Schrödinger's Cat Explained Simply

Picture this: a cat sits in a closed box. Inside there is also a radioactive atom, a detector and a vial of poison. If the atom decays, the detector triggers the mechanism, the poison is released and the cat dies. If it does not decay, the cat lives on. Nobody can see inside, and that is the whole point of the set-up.

|Cat⟩ = 1/√2 · ( |alive⟩ + |dead⟩ ) As long as nobody looks, quantum mechanics describes the atom — and therefore the cat too — as a superposition of both states at once

As long as the box stays closed, quantum mechanics says both possibilities are simultaneously "true" — only looking inside decides which reality occurs.

💬 Mia asks Grandpa Theo

Mia

Wait — is the cat really alive AND dead at the same time until you look inside?

Grandpa Theo

That is exactly the point. Schrödinger came up with this in 1935 not because he believed it, but to show how absurd it sounds when you apply the rules of the quantum world directly to an everyday object like a cat.

Mia

But where does the idea of superposition even come from?

Grandpa Theo

Very small particles like atoms and electrons really do behave that way — this is not nonsense. Before you measure, several possibilities exist at once as probabilities. Only the measurement "decides" on an outcome. For tiny particles this is well established. In everyday life, with big things like cats, it practically never happens.

Mia

Why doesn't it happen with the cat then — she's made of atoms too?

Grandpa Theo

Because a cat is made of countless billions of particles that are constantly interacting with their surroundings — air molecules, light, heat. Each of those interactions acts almost like a tiny measurement. The superposition collapses within a fraction of a second, long before anyone opens the box. Physicists call this decoherence.

Not just theory

Superposition is not merely a thought exercise. Modern quantum computers use exactly this principle. Unlike an ordinary bit, which is either 0 or 1, a "qubit" can hold both states at once for as long as it is not read out.

Key terms

Superposition — a quantum system is in several states at once as long as it is not measured.
Measurement — the interaction that pins the system down to one state.
Decoherence — the loss of superposition through contact with the surroundings.
Thought experiment — a set-up that is only ever played out in the mind.

What Schrödinger actually meant

The experiment is not a claim, it is a criticism. In 1935 Schrödinger found the then-standard interpretation of quantum mechanics unsatisfying, and he used the cat to show how absurd it becomes when you carry it over to large objects. A cat that is alive and dead at the same time was meant as an accusation, not as a description of reality. The irony is that the image became the most famous picture of quantum physics precisely because it was supposed to be ridiculous.

This is where the modern answer lies: decoherence. A large object is permanently interacting with its surroundings — through air molecules, thermal radiation, light. Every one of those interactions works like a measurement. The superposition falls apart so quickly that it never becomes observable. No human observer is required at any point, which removes the mystical flavour the story often gets.

Common mistakes

"The cat really is both." The experiment was designed to show that this idea does not hold up.
Consciousness as the trigger for measurement. Any interaction with the surroundings is enough.
Carrying quantum effects into everyday life. Your keys are not in superposition until you look for them.
"Physicists don't know." The calculations are undisputed and confirmed millions of times over. The argument is about interpretation, not about the predictions.

Practice question

Use the idea of decoherence to explain why superposition can be measured for atoms in a laboratory but not for a cat.

Show solution

A single atom can be almost completely isolated from its surroundings: in a vacuum, at very low temperatures and shielded from light. Hardly any interactions take place, so the superposition survives long enough to be demonstrated through interference patterns. This has been achieved with molecules made of several hundred atoms.

A cat consists of roughly 1026 particles and constantly exchanges heat, air molecules and photons with its surroundings. Each of those processes carries information about its state to the outside world and acts like a measurement. The superposition therefore collapses in an unimaginably short time — on the order of 10−30 seconds and below. The transition from quantum behaviour to everyday behaviour is not a matter of principle, then, but a matter of isolation.
