Mass and Density Explained Simply

Why does a steel nail sink, but a steel ship does not? The answer is not the weight but the density — that is, how much mass is packed into a given volume.

ρ = m / V density = mass divided by volume · unit g/cm³ or kg/m³
Key terms

Mass (m) — the amount of matter, in grams or kilograms. The same everywhere in the universe.
Weight — the force with which the mass is attracted, in newtons. Smaller on the moon, while the mass stays the same.
Volume (V) — the space taken up, in cm³ or m³.
Density (ρ) — mass per volume; the symbol is the Greek letter rho.
Buoyancy — the upward force, as large as the weight of the displaced liquid.

A kilogram of feathers and a kilogram of lead

Both weigh the same — the famous joke. The difference is the volume: a kilogram of lead fits in a fist, a kilogram of feathers fills a sack. Density captures exactly this relationship in a single number.

The rule is simple: anything with a lower density than the liquid floats. Ice, at 0.92 g/cm³, sits just below water — which is why ice in fresh water sticks out only about eight percent, and an iceberg in the sea about ten percent.

A cube weighs 216 g and has an edge length of 3 cm.

V = 3 · 3 · 3 = 27 cm³
ρ = 216 ÷ 27 = 8.0 g/cm³

Compare with the table → that is iron, not gold.

💬 Mia asks Grandpa Theo

Mia

But a steel ship is much heavier than a nail!

Grandpa Theo

True. But it is not the weight that counts, it is the weight per space. A ship is hollow inside, and most of what is in there is air.

Mia

So the density of the whole ship, not the density of the steel?

Grandpa Theo

Exactly. Divide the mass by the entire volume including the hollow space — and you end up below 1 g/cm³. That is why it floats.

Common mistakes

Mixing up mass and weight. Mass in kg, weight in newtons. On the moon you weigh less, but your mass stays the same.
Mixing units. Grams with cubic meters does not work. Before dividing, convert everything to g and cm³ or to kg and m³. Remember: 1 g/cm³ = 1000 kg/m³.
"Heavy means it sinks." A tree trunk is heavy and still floats.
Forgetting hollow spaces. For floating, the entire displaced volume counts, not just the material.

Practice question

A piece of jewelry has a mass of 96.5 g. Placed in a completely full container of water, 5 cm³ spill over. Is it made of gold? Give a reason.

Show solution

ρ = 96.5 g ÷ 5 cm³ = 19.3 g/cm³
That matches the density of gold exactly. Caution is still called for: tungsten has almost the same value at 19.25 g/cm³ and is used for forgeries. Measuring the density is a strong indication, but not proof — that needs an additional test, for example of the electrical conductivity.

Density in everyday life

Once your eye is trained, you see density everywhere — and mostly where something floats, sinks or rises.

A hot-air balloon does not rise because warm air is "light" but because air needs more room when it is heated. The mass stays the same, the volume grows, so the density falls. As soon as the density inside the balloon is lower than that of the surrounding air, it rises — the same principle as with the ship, only with air instead of water. A drop of oil floats on water because oil, at about 0.92 g/cm³, is less dense than water at 1.00 g/cm³. And the fact that ice floats is a real oddity: almost all substances become denser when they solidify, but water does not. Ice has 0.917 g/cm³ and therefore floats with about a tenth of its volume above the surface — the rest lies below, hence the saying about the tip of the iceberg.

Why salt water holds you up better

In the Dead Sea you can lie on your back and read without sinking. The reason is density again, this time that of the liquid. Fresh water has about 1.00 g/cm³, ordinary seawater about 1.025 g/cm³, and the Dead Sea about 1.24 g/cm³ — it contains roughly ten times as much dissolved salt as a normal ocean. Depending on the share of fat and muscle, a human body has a density of roughly 0.95 to 1.05 g/cm³, which is very close to the value of water. That is why the air in your lungs often decides whether you float or sink: whoever breathes in deeply lowers their average density and rises, whoever breathes out sinks. In the Dead Sea the difference is so large that the body cannot go under at all. Cargo ships use exactly the same principle: a mark on the hull shows how deep the ship may sink in fresh or salt water, because in a freshwater port the same ship sits noticeably lower than at sea.

The method with the overflowing water goes back to Archimedes. Its trick: for irregularly shaped objects there is no volume formula — the displaced water measures the volume anyway.
