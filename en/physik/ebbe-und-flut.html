Physics - Tides

Tides Explained Simply

Twice a day the water rises, twice a day it falls. The usual explanation, "the moon pulls the water towards it," only accounts for one bulge. So why is there high tide on the side of the Earth facing away from the moon at the same time? That is the real question.

The moon does not only pull on the water, it pulls on the whole Earth — just not equally hard everywhere. Tides come from the difference in the pull, not from the pull itself
Key terms

Tidal force — the difference between the pull at one place and the pull at the center of the Earth.
Tidal bulge — the swelling of the water; there are always two, opposite each other.
Tidal range — the difference in height between high water and low water.
Spring tide — an especially large range at new and full moon, because sun and moon pull along one line.
Neap tide — a weak range at half moon, because sun and moon stand at right angles.

Why there are two tidal bulges

Gravitational pull gets weaker with distance. The water on the moon's side is closest to the moon and is pulled hardest, so it bulges towards the moon. The center of the Earth is farther away and is pulled less. The water on the far side is the farthest away and is pulled least of all.

What matters is the comparison: on the far side the Earth is pulled away from under the water. The water is, in a sense, left behind, and from the outside that looks like a second bulge. That is why there are two tidal bulges, and why there are two high tides a day.

Where 12 hours 25 minutes comes from

The Earth turns once around itself in 24 h.
In that time the moon moves on a little (orbit: 27.3 days).

Until the same place is under the moon again: 24 h 50 min
Two tidal bulges per orbit → 24 h 50 min ÷ 2 = 12 h 25 min

That is why high water shifts back by about 50 minutes every day. Anyone who reads a tide table on the North Sea coast sees exactly this pattern.

💬 Mia asks Grandpa Theo

Mia

If the moon pulls the water towards it, why is there high tide on the other side too?

Grandpa Theo

Because it also pulls on the Earth itself, and harder than on the water behind it. The Earth is pulled away from under the water on the far side.

Mia

And why don't you notice it in a swimming pool?

Grandpa Theo

Because only the difference over the distance counts. Over the twelve meters of a pool it is vanishingly small. Over the 12,700 kilometers of the Earth's diameter it is not.

Common mistakes

"One side of the Earth is closer to the moon, so there is high tide." That explains only one bulge, not two.
Centrifugal force as the only explanation. It shows up in older textbooks, but it is a makeshift construction — the difference in gravitational pull is completely sufficient.
Forgetting the sun. It is far more massive but much farther away; its tidal effect is only about 46 % of the moon's.
Assuming the tidal range is the same everywhere. In the North Sea two to four meters is normal, in the Bay of Fundy over fifteen, in the Mediterranean a few centimeters — the shape of the coast amplifies or dampens it.

Practice question

On Monday there is high water at 6:10 a.m. When is the third high water after that? Use 12 h 25 min and explain why the time shifts every day.

Show solution

1st high water: 6:10 a.m. + 12:25 = 6:35 p.m.
2nd high water: 6:35 p.m. + 12:25 = 7:00 a.m. on Tuesday
3rd high water: 7:00 a.m. + 12:25 = 7:25 p.m. on Tuesday
The shift happens because the moon itself keeps moving during one rotation of the Earth. The Earth has to turn about 50 minutes longer until the same place is under the moon again — so about 25 minutes per tide.

Where tides are noticeable — and where they are not

Tidal forces act everywhere on Earth, but they become visible mainly in the oceans. Small seas like the Baltic are connected to the Atlantic only through narrow passages and are too small for a tidal bulge to build up: the tidal range there is usually only a few centimeters, and wind and air pressure change the water level more than the moon does. The Mediterranean, too, has only a small range.

The North Sea is different. The tidal wave enters from the Atlantic, is slowed and piled up on the shallow shelf, and the range grows to several meters. In the Bay of Fundy the bay is also shaped so that its water swings in step with the tides, like a swing that is pushed at just the right rhythm. Some of the largest tidal ranges in the world occur there.

And one more thing: it is not only the water that reacts to the moon. The solid Earth also rises and falls in step with the tidal force, by up to about 30 centimeters. We do not notice it because the ground, houses, roads and measuring devices all move together — only very sensitive instruments can detect it.

Tides slow down the Earth's rotation measurably: the day grows longer by about 1.8 milliseconds per century, and the moon moves away from us by roughly 3.8 centimeters per year. Both have been measured directly with reflectors left on the moon.
