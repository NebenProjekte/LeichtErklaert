Water Displacement Explained Simply

Written by Viktor Jonas · Last checked on 22 October 2026 · Reading time about 8 minutes

When you lower an object into water, it pushes the water aside — it displaces it. The displaced water needs room as well and has to go somewhere, so the water level rises. That simple observation is the starting point for one of the oldest pieces of physics we still use every day.

Buoyancy = weight of the displaced water an object floats if it displaces at least as much water as it weighs itself (Archimedes' principle)

Whether something floats or sinks does not depend on weight alone but on density. A huge steel ship floats because its shape displaces an enormous amount of water — the buoyancy from that displaced water is exactly as large as the weight of the ship. A small solid stone, by contrast, displaces far too little water compared with its weight, and down it goes.

💬 Mia asks Grandpa Theo

Mia

But a steel ship is incredibly heavy — steel normally sinks, doesn't it?

Grandpa Theo

A solid lump of steel does sink. But a ship is hollow inside — it is really almost all air, wrapped in a thin steel shell. That gives it a huge volume compared with its weight.

Mia

Ah, so it isn't just the weight that counts, but how much room the thing takes up?

Grandpa Theo

Exactly — that is what density means: weight per volume. And the trick behind it is more than 2000 years old. Archimedes discovered that an object receives exactly as much buoyancy as the water it pushes aside weighs. If it displaces more weight in water than it weighs itself, it floats.

Try it yourself

Fill a bowl to the brim with water and lower a glass into it. The water that spills over weighs exactly as much as the buoyancy the glass receives — you can measure that directly on a kitchen scale.

Submarines use precisely this principle: they flood tanks with water to become heavier and sink, then blow the tanks empty with air to rise again. There is more on the link between weight and volume under mass and density.

Key terms

Buoyancy — the upward force on a submerged body.
Archimedes' principle — the buoyancy equals the weight of the displaced fluid.
Displaced volume — the volume of the part of the body that is under water.
Floating — buoyancy equals weight, the body is only partly submerged.
Neutral buoyancy — both forces are equal while the body is fully submerged.

The formula and what it tells you

FB = ρfluid · Vdisplaced · g

Example: a body displaces 2 litres of water
F = 1000 kg/m³ · 0.002 m³ · 9.81 = 19.6 N
that matches the weight of 2 kg of water

What is striking is what does not appear in the formula: the mass of the body and the depth. A brick experiences the same buoyancy at two metres as at twenty. The only thing that matters is how much fluid it pushes aside. Divers feel this the moment they stop sinking faster the deeper they go.

Common mistakes

Thinking buoyancy grows with depth
It stays the same as long as the body is fully submerged.
Using total volume instead of displaced volume
For a floating body only the submerged part counts.
Forgetting the density of the fluid
Buoyancy is greater in salt water — which is why floating in the Dead Sea takes no effort.
Putting the mass of the body into the formula
It is about the volume and the density of the fluid.
Practice question

A block of wood (density 0.6 g/cm³) with a volume of 1000 cm³ floats in water. What fraction sticks out? How does that change in sea water with a density of 1.03 g/cm³?

Show solution

At equilibrium: submerged fraction = ρbody ÷ ρfluid
Fresh water: 0.6 ÷ 1.0 = 0.6 → 60 % submerged, so 40 % sticks out (600 cm³ under water).

Sea water: 0.6 ÷ 1.03 = 0.583 → 58.3 % submerged, so 41.7 % sticks out.

The block therefore rides slightly higher in salt water. The same calculation explains why ships sit deeper in a freshwater harbour than at sea — and it is why the load line painted on a hull is marked separately for different waters.

The ice cube in a full glass

A glass is filled to the brim with water and an ice cube floats in it. Will it overflow when the ice melts? The surprising answer is no. The floating cube already displaces exactly as much water as it weighs. When it melts it turns into that same mass of liquid water — and that takes up precisely the volume it had displaced before. The level stays where it was. The same holds for Arctic sea ice: its melting barely raises sea level at all. Glaciers and the ice sheets on Greenland and Antarctica are a different matter — that ice sits on land and displaces no water beforehand. Only its meltwater raises the level.

Practice: five questions, instant feedback

Click an answer. You see straight away whether it is right — and why.

  1. What does the buoyant force depend on?

    F = ρ · V · g. Neither the object's mass nor the depth appears in the formula — once fully submerged, the buoyancy at ten metres is the same as just below the surface.

  2. An object with a density of 0.8 g/cm³ floats in water. How much of it is below the surface?

    80 per cent. The submerged fraction is the density of the object divided by the density of the liquid: 0.8 ÷ 1.0.

  3. Why does a steel ship float while a lump of steel does not?

    What counts is average density. A hollow hull is mostly air, so the whole ship is less dense than water. The same steel as a solid lump has about 7.8 g/cm³ and sinks.

  4. An ice cube floats in a glass filled to the brim. What happens when it melts?

    The level stays put. While floating, the ice displaces exactly the volume of water it turns into when it melts.

  5. A ship sails from the sea into a freshwater harbour. What happens?

    Fresh water at 1.0 g/cm³ is less dense than seawater at about 1.025. To get the same buoyant force the ship must displace more volume, so it sits deeper. That is why the load line marking has separate lines for fresh and salt water.

Exam-style questions with model answers

Work them out first, then open the answer.

An aluminium cube (density 2.7 g/cm³) with 10 cm edges hangs fully submerged in water. Find its weight, the buoyant force and the force on the rope.

Volume: V = 10 cm · 10 cm · 10 cm = 1000 cm³ = 0.001 m³.

Mass: m = 2700 kg/m³ · 0.001 m³ = 2.7 kg. Weight: FG = 2.7 kg · 9.81 m/s² ≈ 26.5 N.

Buoyancy: FA = 1000 kg/m³ · 0.001 m³ · 9.81 m/s² ≈ 9.81 N.

The rope carries the difference: 26.5 N − 9.81 N ≈ 16.7 N. Submerged, the cube appears lighter by exactly the weight of one litre of water.

Explain why the mass of the object does not appear in the formula for buoyancy.

Buoyancy comes from the pressure difference between the bottom and the top of the submerged object. That difference depends only on how tall the column of liquid above each surface is and how dense the liquid is — that is, on the displaced volume, not on what fills it.

Put differently: the liquid cannot tell what occupies the space the object keeps free. A polystyrene cube gets exactly the same buoyant force as an identical lead cube. Whether it floats is decided only by comparing that force with its own weight.

A ship has a mass of 20,000 t. How much water does it displace, and how does fresh water differ from seawater?

A floating ship always displaces its own weight in water: 20,000 t, or 20,000,000 kg.

In fresh water (1000 kg/m³): V = 20,000,000 ÷ 1000 = 20,000 m³. In seawater (about 1025 kg/m³): V = 20,000,000 ÷ 1025 ≈ 19,512 m³.

The difference of roughly 488 m³ has to go below the waterline in fresh water, so the ship sits measurably deeper. Hence the separate fresh-water marks on the load line.
