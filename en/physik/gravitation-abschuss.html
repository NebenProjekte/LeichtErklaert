Gravity Explained Simply: Why a Fired Bullet Lands at the Same Time as a Dropped One

Imagine this: you fire a bullet horizontally from a pistol. At the same moment someone next to you simply drops a second bullet from the same height. Which bullet reaches the ground first?

Answer: both at the same time (if you ignore air resistance, as is usually done in physics class).

The four dots on the left show the dropped bullet at four equally spaced moments, the four dots along the curve show the fired bullet at the same moments. At every moment both have fallen exactly the same distance — the fired bullet just also moves to the right.

Why is that?

A movement through space can be split into two independent directions: horizontal and vertical.

  • Horizontal: The fired bullet simply flies straight ahead at constant speed (no force acts in that direction if you ignore air resistance).
  • Vertical: Exactly the same gravitational force of the Earth pulls both bullets downwards — regardless of whether or how fast they move horizontally.

Gravity "knows nothing" about the bullet's horizontal speed. It pulls both bullets down at exactly the same rate. That is why both bullets cover the same falling distance in the same time — and hit the ground together.

Falling distance: s = ½ · g · t² applies equally to both bullets, regardless of the horizontal speed

💬 Mia asks Grandpa Theo

Mia

Wait — why doesn't the fired bullet fall faster than the one I just let go? It starts out so much faster!

Grandpa Theo

Yes, but only sideways! Downwards, gravity pulls both bullets exactly equally hard. The fast sideways motion changes nothing about that.

Mia

Wow, I would never have guessed that! Does it have a name?

Grandpa Theo

Physicists call it the independence of the directions of motion. Horizontal and vertical are like two separate stories that play out at the same time!

Note: in the real world, air resistance slows fast bullets down a little, which changes the result slightly. For physics class (and almost all everyday cases), though, air resistance is ignored — the principle stays the same.

Key terms

Gravitation — the mutual attraction of all masses.
First cosmic velocity — 7.9 km/s, needed for a low orbit around the Earth.
Escape velocity — 11.2 km/s, needed to leave the Earth's gravitational field.
Orbit — a permanent free fall in which the ground curves away just as fast.
Weightlessness — not an absence of gravity, but free fall.

Newton's thought experiment

Imagine a cannon on a very high mountain. At a low muzzle velocity the ball drops to the ground after a few kilometers. The faster it is, the farther it flies. At about 7.9 km/s the Earth's surface curves away exactly as fast as the ball falls — it never lands and circles the Earth instead. An orbit is therefore nothing other than a fall that keeps missing the ground.

How far does the bullet fly? A worked example

The independence of the directions of motion can be calculated. The falling time depends only on the height, while the range depends on the falling time and the horizontal speed.

A bullet is fired horizontally at 400 m/s from a height of 1.5 m (no air resistance).

Falling time: t = √(2 · h ÷ g) = √(2 · 1.5 ÷ 9.81) = 0.55 s
Range: x = v · t = 400 · 0.553 = about 221 m

A bullet simply dropped from a height of 1.5 m also takes 0.55 s.

In reality a projectile flies a shorter distance because of air resistance, but the falling time stays practically the same. What matters is the difference between the two directions: the vertical speed on impact is g · t = 5.4 m/s for both bullets. The horizontal speed of 400 m/s changes nothing about that; it only determines how far the bullet gets before it hits the ground.

Common mistakes

"There is no gravity in space." At an altitude of 400 km, about 90 % of the Earth's pull still acts. Astronauts are weightless because they are falling — not because nothing is pulling.
Mixing up height and speed. What decides an orbit is the sideways speed, not the altitude.
Ignoring air resistance. That is why no rocket launches straight up to orbital speed but tilts over gradually.
Reading escape velocity as a permanent speed. It applies to a single thrust with no further propulsion.

Practice question

Explain why astronauts on the ISS float even though almost the full pull of the Earth acts there.

Show solution

The ISS orbits the Earth at an altitude of about 400 km. There the gravitational acceleration is still about 8.7 m/s², roughly 89 % of the value at the ground. So gravity is by no means missing.

The station and its crew are in free fall around the Earth: they are constantly falling but miss the ground, because their sideways speed of about 7.7 km/s compensates for the curvature of the Earth. Since everything falls at the same rate — station, person, glass of water — there is no supporting force between them. That supporting force is exactly what we normally perceive as weight. The correct term is therefore microgravity, not weightlessness.
