Volts vs Amps vs Watts Explained Simply

Written by Viktor Jonas · Last checked on 22 October 2026 · Reading time about 8 minutes

Volts, amps and watts describe three completely different things, yet they get mixed up constantly — on packaging, in shop descriptions and in everyday conversation. The quickest way to keep them apart is to picture a water pipe. Once that image sits, the formulas stop being something you memorise and start being something you can reconstruct.

P = U · I power (watts) = voltage (volts) · current (amps)
Key terms

Voltage U in volts (V) — the pressure that pushes the charges along. In the water picture: the water pressure.
Current I in amps (A) — how many charges flow past per second. In the water picture: the flow rate.
Resistance R in ohms (Ω) — how strongly the conductor holds the flow back. In the water picture: how narrow the pipe is.
Power P in watts (W) — the energy converted per second.
Energy W in watt-hours (Wh) or kilowatt-hours (kWh) — power multiplied by time. This is what your electricity bill charges you for.

The water picture

A narrow pipe at high pressure can deliver the same amount of water per minute as a wide pipe at low pressure. Electricity behaves the same way: 230 V at 0.5 A gives exactly the same power as 12 V at almost 10 A — roughly 115 watts in both cases. That is why the voltage on its own tells you nothing about how much a device actually does. A 12-volt car starter is far more powerful than a 230-volt reading lamp.

That last row explains why jumper cables are so thick. It is not the voltage that demands a fat copper cross-section — it is the current. Every amp that flows heats the wire, so the more amps, the more metal you need to carry them without the cable glowing.

Ohm's law ties the three together

U = R · I  |  I = U ÷ R  |  R = U ÷ I

Example: a 2000 W kettle running on 230 V
I = P ÷ U = 2000 ÷ 230 = 8.7 A
R = U ÷ I = 230 ÷ 8.7 = 26.4 Ω

Notice how you only ever need two of the values to find the third. In an exam, that is usually the whole task: you are handed the power rating printed on the device and the mains voltage of the country, and everything else follows from those two numbers.

💬 Mia asks Grandpa Theo

Mia

Why is a wall socket dangerous when a 12-volt car battery isn't?

Grandpa Theo

Because what hurts you is the current through your body. Twelve volts cannot push enough current through dry skin. Two hundred and thirty volts can.

Mia

But the battery can do 200 amps!

Grandpa Theo

It can supply that much. What actually flows is decided by the voltage and the resistance in the way. What is on offer is not the same as what gets used.

Watts and watt-hours are not the same thing

This is where most confusion on electricity bills comes from. Watts tell you how fast energy is being used right now. Watt-hours tell you how much has been used over a stretch of time. A 2000-watt kettle is a monster while it runs, but it only runs for three minutes. A 100-watt fridge is modest, but it never stops. Over a month the quiet fridge wins by a wide margin.

Common mistakes

Treating volts as power. Volts are pressure, watts are power. A high voltage with almost no current delivers almost nothing.
Treating amps as a property of the device. The current follows from the voltage and the resistance. It is not a fixed label.
Confusing watts and watt-hours. Watts are the rate, watt-hours are the total. Bills are in kilowatt-hours.
Mixing units in a calculation. Convert milliamps to amps first, or your answer will be out by a factor of 1000.

Practice question

A 2000 W fan heater runs for 3 hours a day. A kilowatt-hour costs 0.30 €. Work out the current at 230 V and the cost over 30 days.

Show solution

Current: I = P ÷ U = 2000 ÷ 230 = 8.7 A
Energy per day: 2000 W · 3 h = 6000 Wh = 6 kWh
Energy over 30 days: 6 · 30 = 180 kWh
Cost: 180 · 0.30 € = 54 €
For comparison: a 9 W LED lamp burning for the same three hours a day uses 0.81 kWh a month, about 24 cents. Heating with electricity is expensive not because of the voltage but because of the sheer power involved.

Rule of thumb for fuses: a 16 A household fuse at 230 V tolerates roughly 3680 watts. Two kettles on the same circuit are already too much — which is why the breaker trips before the wiring gets hot.

Practice: five questions, instant feedback

Click an answer. You see straight away whether it is right — and why.

  1. Which quantity tells you how much a device actually does?

    Power. Voltage on its own says nothing: 230 V at 0.5 A and 12 V at almost 10 A deliver roughly the same 115 watts.

  2. A 2000 W kettle runs on 230 V. How much current flows?

    I = P ÷ U = 2000 ÷ 230 ≈ 8.7 A. That is why kettles sit close to the limit of a normal household circuit.

  3. Why is a wall socket dangerous while a 12 V car battery is not, even though the battery can supply 200 A?

    What matters is the current through you, and that depends on voltage and your body's resistance. A battery can supply 200 A, but 12 V across a body resistance of several thousand ohms drives only a few milliamps.

  4. What is the difference between a watt and a watt-hour?

    Watts tell you how fast energy is used, watt-hours how much was used in total. Your bill charges for kilowatt-hours, not for watts.

  5. A lamp draws 0.5 A at 230 V. What is its resistance?

    R = U ÷ I = 230 ÷ 0.5 = 460 ohms. The power is U · I = 115 W.

Exam-style questions with model answers

Try them yourself first, then open the answer.

A hairdryer is rated 1800 W at 230 V. Calculate the current, the resistance and the cost of using it for 10 minutes a day over a month at 0.30 € per kWh.

Current: I = P ÷ U = 1800 ÷ 230 ≈ 7.8 A.

Resistance: R = U ÷ I = 230 ÷ 7.8 ≈ 29.4 ohms.

Energy: 1800 W is 1.8 kW. Ten minutes is one sixth of an hour, so 0.3 kWh per day, about 9 kWh in a 30-day month.

Cost: 9 kWh · 0.30 € ≈ 2.70 € per month. The useful habit here is converting minutes into hours before multiplying — that is where most marks are lost.

Explain why thick jumper cables are needed for a 12 V car battery, while a thin cable is enough for a 230 V lamp.

Cable thickness is set by current, not by voltage. A lamp at 230 V and 115 W draws about 0.5 A; starting an engine briefly pulls several hundred amps from the battery.

A conductor heats up with the square of the current (P = I² · R), so a hundredfold current means a ten-thousandfold heating in the same wire. The cable must therefore have a far larger cross-section to keep its own resistance and its heating small.

Voltage matters for insulation instead: 230 V needs thicker insulation, 12 V barely any.

Two devices are rated 12 V / 60 W and 230 V / 60 W. Compare current and resistance, and say what that means in practice.

At 12 V: I = 60 ÷ 12 = 5 A, R = 12 ÷ 5 = 2.4 ohms. At 230 V: I = 60 ÷ 230 ≈ 0.26 A, R = 230 ÷ 0.26 ≈ 882 ohms.

Same power, but the low-voltage device pulls roughly twenty times the current and needs much thicker conductors and connectors. The high-voltage device needs better insulation instead.

This trade-off is exactly why long-distance power lines run at hundreds of thousands of volts: the higher the voltage, the lower the current for the same power, and the smaller the losses in the line.
