Falling Speed Explained Simply

If you simply let go of an object without throwing it, gravity makes it faster and faster as it falls. Physicists call this free fall. The useful part is that the increase follows a very simple rule, so you can predict the speed at any moment with a single multiplication.

v = g · t v = speed after time t, g = acceleration due to gravity ≈ 9.81 m/s²

The falling speed therefore rises evenly with time. Every extra second adds roughly another 9.81 m/s.

Example: speed

After t = 1 s: v = 9.81 · 1 = 9.81 m/s (about 35 km/h)

After t = 2 s: v = 9.81 · 2 = 19.62 m/s (about 71 km/h)

After t = 3 s: v = 9.81 · 3 = 29.43 m/s (about 106 km/h)

If you want to know how far something has fallen in that time, you need a second formula:

s = ½ · g · t² s = fall distance in metres
Example: distance

After t = 2 s: s = 0.5 · 9.81 · 2² = 0.5 · 9.81 · 4 = 19.62 m

So after two seconds of free fall an object has already dropped almost 20 metres — and it keeps speeding up.

💬 Mia asks Grandpa Theo

Mia

Do you just keep getting faster and faster forever if you fall long enough?

Grandpa Theo

Only in theory, with no air. In reality the air pushes back harder and harder until the braking force and gravity balance each other out.

Mia

Ah, like skydiving, right?

Grandpa Theo

Exactly. That balance point is called terminal velocity — pretty fast without a parachute, and thankfully far slower with one.

The fact that the speed grows so evenly is directly connected to the law of conservation of energy: the potential energy from up high is converted step by step into kinetic energy.

Key terms

Gravitational acceleration g — 9.81 m/s² in Central Europe. Every second adds another 9.81 m/s.
Free fall — falling without air resistance. Only exactly true in a vacuum.
Air resistance — the braking force; it grows with the square of the speed.
Terminal velocity — reached when air resistance and weight are equal. After that the body falls at a constant speed.

The two formulas side by side

v = g · t   |   s = ½ · g · t²

after 1 s: v = 9.81 m/s, s = 4.9 m
after 2 s: v = 19.6 m/s, s = 19.6 m
after 3 s: v = 29.4 m/s, s = 44.1 m

The distance grows with the square of the time. During the third second a body covers 24.5 metres, during the first only 4.9 — five times as much. That is exactly why falls from greater heights are disproportionately dangerous rather than just a bit worse.

One more detail worth knowing: g is not quite the same everywhere on Earth. At the poles it is about 9.83 m/s², at the equator about 9.78, because the planet bulges outwards there and you sit slightly further from its centre. School exercises almost always use 9.81, and rough mental estimates often use 10, which makes the numbers easy and is accurate to within two per cent.

Common mistakes

"Heavy things fall faster." In a vacuum a hammer and a feather fall at the same rate — it was demonstrated on the Moon in 1971.
Mixing up distance and speed. v grows linearly, s grows quadratically.
Forgetting air resistance. Without it a skydiver would reach 2100 km/h after 60 seconds.
Reading g as a speed. It is an acceleration, with units of m/s².

Practice question

A stone falls from a bridge and hits the water after 2.5 s. How high is the bridge, and how fast is the stone on impact? Ignore air resistance.

Show solution

s = ½ · 9.81 · 2.5² = ½ · 9.81 · 6.25 = 30.7 m
v = 9.81 · 2.5 = 24.5 m/s, which is about 88 km/h.

For perspective: air resistance would reduce the real impact speed by only a few per cent. For compact, heavy objects and short fall times it is therefore fair to ignore it — for a sheet of paper it would be badly wrong.

The same square in road traffic: stopping distance

The rule "double the time, four times the distance" also shows up in cars. The stopping distance has two parts: the reaction distance, which the car covers without braking while the driver is still reacting, and the braking distance. The reaction distance grows steadily with speed, while the braking distance grows with its square: s = v² ÷ (2 · a). This has the same structure as free fall, except that the braking deceleration a takes the place of g.

Assumptions: reaction time 1 s, braking deceleration a = 8 m/s² (dry road, good tyres, a rough approximation)

50 km/h = 13.9 m/s: reaction distance 13.9 m, braking distance 13.9² ÷ 16 = 12.1 m, stopping distance about 26 m
100 km/h = 27.8 m/s: reaction distance 27.8 m, braking distance 27.8² ÷ 16 = 48.3 m, stopping distance about 76 m

At double the speed the braking distance therefore becomes four times as long, and the whole stopping distance almost three times as long. On a wet road a is much smaller, and the distance grows accordingly.

Why small animals survive falls more easily

A mouse can fall from heights that would seriously injure a person. The reason is air resistance again. If an animal shrinks evenly in every direction, its cross-sectional area falls with the square of its length, but its mass falls with the cube. Halve the body length and the animal keeps only a quarter of the area but an eighth of the mass: per kilogram, the air pushes back twice as hard.

That is why small bodies have a lower terminal velocity. It grows roughly with the square root of the body length, so an animal ten times smaller reaches only about a third of the terminal velocity. Since impact energy rises with the square of the speed, the difference on landing is large. The table above shows the same pattern: the small raindrop stays at about 6 m/s, while the much larger skydiver reaches about 55 m/s.

The same mathematics governs braking distance

Distance growing with the square of time is not peculiar to falling. Braking a car follows the same pattern, just with the sign reversed. That is why driving instructors teach two rules of thumb which capture exactly this.

Reaction distance = (speed ÷ 10) · 3
Braking distance = (speed ÷ 10)²

at 50 km/h: 15 m + 25 m = 40 m
at 100 km/h: 30 m + 100 m = 130 m

Twice the speed therefore gives not twice but more than three times the total stopping distance. The reaction distance grows linearly while the braking distance grows quadratically — and from about 60 km/h upwards the second clearly overtakes the first.

Why small animals survive falls

A person falling ten metres is badly hurt. A mouse usually walks away from the same drop. The reason lies in the ratio of air resistance to mass. Air resistance depends on cross-sectional area and grows with the square of body length, while mass grows with the cube. Halve an animal's size and its mass drops to an eighth while the braking area drops only to a quarter. Small animals therefore reach a far lower terminal velocity: a mouse tops out around 13 m/s, a human around 55 m/s. From the same height the mouse lands with a fraction of the energy per kilogram.
