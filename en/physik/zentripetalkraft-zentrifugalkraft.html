Centripetal Force and Centrifugal Force Explained Simply

In a curve you feel pushed outwards — at least that is how it feels. Physically the opposite is happening: a force acts inwards. Everything else is inertia.

Fc = m · v² / r centripetal force · mass times speed squared, divided by the radius
Key terms

Centripetal force — the real force acting towards the center of the circle. Without it there would be no curve.
Centrifugal force — the felt force outwards. A fictitious force that only appears in the co-moving frame of reference.
Inertia — every body stays at rest or in uniform straight-line motion as long as no force acts on it.
Frame of reference — the standpoint from which something is described: from outside or riding along.

Why the force points inwards

A body moves straight ahead by itself. For it to follow a curve, something has to keep pulling it away from that straight line — for a car it is the friction of the tires, for the moon it is gravity, for the hammer throw it is the cable. If the cable snaps, the hammer does not fly off outwards but straight ahead along the tangent. That is exactly what proves that there was no force directed outwards.

Car: m = 1200 kg, v = 20 m/s (72 km/h), r = 50 m

F = 1200 · 20² ÷ 50 = 1200 · 400 ÷ 50 = 9600 N

Double the speed, 40 m/s:
F = 1200 · 1600 ÷ 50 = 38,400 N — four times as much

The square in the formula is the most important part: twice as fast means four times the force. That is why curve accidents at high speed are so much more severe, and why tight curves have low speed limits.

💬 Mia asks Grandpa Theo

Mia

But I am clearly pushed outwards in a curve. I am not imagining that!

Grandpa Theo

You want to keep going straight — that is inertia. The car door comes towards you and pushes you inwards. What you feel is the pressure of the door, not a force outwards.

Mia

Then why do people say centrifugal force?

Grandpa Theo

Because in the co-moving frame you are allowed to calculate that way. As a calculation aid it is fine; as an explanation it is misleading.

Common mistakes

Taking centrifugal force to be real. It has no cause and no counterpart — both would require a real force.
Forgetting the radius. Half the radius means twice the force.
Not converting km/h. The formula needs m/s, otherwise the result is off by a factor of 13.
Not squaring the speed. The most common calculation error with this formula.

Practice question

A cyclist (bike and rider together 85 kg) rides at 18 km/h through a curve with a radius of 12 m. Calculate the centripetal force required. Static friction provides at most 0.7 · weight — is that enough?

Show solution

18 km/h = 18 ÷ 3.6 = 5 m/s
F = 85 · 5² ÷ 12 = 85 · 25 ÷ 12 = 177 N

Maximum friction: 0.7 · 85 · 9.81 = 584 N
177 N is well below that — the curve is no problem.
At 36 km/h it would be 708 N, which is more than the friction can provide: the bike would slide away. Here too the square shows up — double the speed, four times the force.

Why road curves are built with a slope

Static friction is an unreliable source of force, because on a wet or icy road it becomes weak. That is why curves are tilted inwards. At the right angle, part of the normal force from the road points to the center of the circle and supplies the centripetal force by itself. For the angle of tilt α the following applies: tan α = v² ÷ (g · r).

Country road curve: r = 400 m, v = 72 km/h = 20 m/s

tan α = 20² ÷ (9.81 · 400) = 400 ÷ 3924 = 0.102
α = about 5.8° of tilt

At exactly this speed the car would need no friction at all. If it is slower it slides inwards, if it is faster it slides outwards — then friction is needed again.

The spin cycle of a washing machine

The drum presses the laundry inwards, towards the center of the circle, with the centripetal force. The water, by contrast, can escape through the holes in the drum wall and keeps flying straight ahead — just like the hammer in the throw. It is not centrifugal force that "flings" it out, but inertia.

Drum radius 0.25 m, 1200 revolutions per minute

ω = 2 · π · 1200 ÷ 60 = 125.7 rad/s
a = ω² · r = 125.7² · 0.25 = 3948 m/s²
That is 3948 ÷ 9.81 ≈ 402 g — about 400 times the gravitational acceleration.

In space travel, artificial gravity is meant to be created in exactly this way: a rotating ring pushes the crew inwards, which feels like weight. For 1 g at a comfortable two revolutions per minute, the ring would need a radius of about 220 meters.
