Leverage (The Law of the Lever) Explained Simply

A lever gives you nothing for free. All it does is redistribute — whatever force you save, you pay back in distance. That single sentence is the heart of the whole topic, and it has a name: the golden rule of mechanics.

F₁ · l₁ = F₂ · l₂ force × effort arm = load × load arm · the product is called torque
Key terms

Pivot — the fixed point the lever turns about.
Effort arm — the distance from the pivot to where you push.
Load arm — the distance from the pivot to the load.
Torque — force times lever arm, measured in newton metres (Nm).
Double-sided lever — pivot between effort and load, such as a seesaw.
Single-sided lever — effort and load on the same side, such as a wheelbarrow.

Why length matters so much

A stone of 300 kg weighs about 2943 N.
Load arm 0.5 m, effort arm 2.5 m.

F = (2943 N · 0.5 m) ÷ 2.5 m = 589 N

That is the equivalent of about 60 kg of body strength — a person can manage it.

The price is written into the golden rule: to raise the stone by one centimetre, the long end has to travel five centimetres. The work done — force times distance — is exactly the same in both cases.

The tweezers are the most instructive case. Here the force is reduced on purpose, because delicacy matters more than strength. A lever can work in both directions.

Mia

Archimedes supposedly said he could move the Earth. Is that actually possible?

Grandpa Theo

On paper, yes. In practice you would need a lever whose long end measures about 100,000 light years — as wide as the Milky Way.

Mia

And how long would I have to push?

Grandpa Theo

To lift it by one centimetre, pushing at one metre per second: roughly 30 trillion years. The formula is right, the world just refuses to cooperate.

Common mistakes

Measuring the lever arm from the load. Both arms are always measured from the pivot.
Using mass instead of force. Convert to newtons with 9.81 m/s² first, or the units will not match.
Treating an angled lever like a straight one. Only the perpendicular distance to the line of force counts.
Believing a lever saves work. It saves force, never work.

Practice question

On a seesaw, a 30 kg child sits on the left, 2.0 m from the pivot. A 40 kg child sits on the right. How far from the pivot must they sit? And how far does the right child move if the left one drops by 40 cm?

Show solution

Balance: 30 kg · 2.0 m = 40 kg · l₂
l₂ = 60 ÷ 40 = 1.5 m
(You can skip converting to newtons, because 9.81 appears on both sides and cancels out.)

The distances follow the ratio of the lever arms: 40 cm · (1.5 ÷ 2.0) = 30 cm. The heavier child moves less far — and that is the golden rule in action.

Levers in tools and in the body

The law of the lever does not just explain seesaws and crowbars. Once you start looking for pivots you find it in almost every tool — and especially often in your own body.

Tools usually go for the force advantage. In a nutcracker, a pipe wrench or a pair of side cutters the pivot sits close to the load and far from your hand. A bolt cutter with 80-centimetre handles and 4-centimetre jaws multiplies your hand force by a factor of 20. The price is the golden rule again: the handles travel 20 centimetres while the jaws close by one.

The body builds bad levers on purpose

In humans it is almost always the other way round. The biceps attaches only about 4 centimetres behind the elbow, while the hand is roughly 32 centimetres away — a ratio of 1 to 8. Hold 5 kilograms in your hand and the biceps has to supply about eight times that, around 40 kilograms of pull. That sounds like poor engineering, but it is deliberate. The golden rule still applies, it is simply being used in reverse. The muscle shortens by one centimetre and the hand travels eight. The body trades force for speed and reach — exactly what matters for throwing, striking and running. An arm with a good force advantage could lift a lot, but it would move at an agonising crawl. The same pattern shows up in the jaw, the calf and the foot, where the Achilles tendon attaches just behind the ankle joint.

There are three classes of lever, depending on where the pivot sits. In a single-sided lever like a nutcracker, effort and load are on the same side. In a double-sided lever like a seesaw they are on opposite sides. And in tweezers the effort sits between pivot and load, so the force is reduced on purpose in exchange for a longer travel and finer control.
