Physics - Energy

The Law of Conservation of Energy Explained Simply

Energy cannot appear out of nothing and it cannot simply vanish. All it ever does is change form or move from one place to another. That is the law of conservation of energy, also known as the first law of thermodynamics. It is one of the very few statements in physics that has never once been found to fail.

E₀ = E₁ energy before = energy after — only the form changes

When wood burns, the chemical energy stored inside it does not disappear. It is converted entirely into heat and light. At the end there is exactly as much energy as there was at the start, just in a different form.

💬 Mia asks Grandpa Theo

Mia

When a campfire finally goes out, is all that energy just gone?

Grandpa Theo

No, it never truly disappears. While the fire burns, the chemical energy in the wood turns into heat that warms you and light that you can see.

Mia

But the next morning everything is cold and dark — so where did the energy go?

Grandpa Theo

It is still there, just spread very thin. The heat flowed into the air and the ground all around until you can no longer feel it. In total there is just as much energy as before — it is simply no longer concentrated in one place.

Mia

Does that apply to other things too, not just fire?

Grandpa Theo

Always. On a swing, your potential energy at the top becomes kinetic energy at the bottom, and back again. A battery turns chemical energy into electrical energy. But nothing is ever created or destroyed — energy is only passed on or converted.

This same principle explains why a falling object keeps getting faster: its potential energy is converted into kinetic energy as it falls, and nothing is lost on the way.

Fun fact

The unit of energy, the joule, is named after the British physicist James Prescott Joule, who proved in the nineteenth century that heat is a form of energy — one of the key discoveries on the road to the conservation law. It is also the reason a perpetual motion machine is impossible: a machine that runs forever with no energy supplied would break this law of nature.

Key terms

Conservation of energy — energy is never lost and never newly created, it only changes form.
Potential energy — E = m · g · h.
Kinetic energy — E = ½ · m · v².
Elastic energy — stored in stretched springs and rubber bands.
Efficiency — the share of energy that arrives in the form you actually wanted.
Degradation — usable energy becomes heat, which cannot be fully converted back.

Why nothing is ever "used up"

In everyday speech we use up electricity. Physically nothing is used up; it is degraded. High-grade electrical energy becomes motion, light and finally heat. The total amount stays exactly the same, but scattered heat is practically no longer usable. That is precisely why no perpetual motion machine can exist — not because engineers have not tried hard enough, but because the accounting never adds up.

A ball (0.2 kg) falls from a height of 5 m.

Potential energy at the top: E = 0.2 · 9.81 · 5 = 9.81 J
just before it lands, all of it is kinetic energy:
9.81 = ½ · 0.2 · v² → v² = 98.1 → v = 9.9 m/s

Common mistakes

"Energy gets used up." It is converted and degraded.
Forgetting friction. It does not make energy disappear, it produces heat.
Not squaring the speed. Twice the speed means four times the kinetic energy — which is why braking distance grows quadratically.
Assuming efficiency above 100 %. Impossible. Heat pumps only look like an exception because they move existing warmth rather than creating it.

Practice question

A car (1200 kg) drives at 50 km/h. Work out its kinetic energy and compare it with the value at 100 km/h. What does that mean for the braking distance?

Show solution

50 km/h = 13.9 m/s → E = ½ · 1200 · 13.9² = 115,900 J
100 km/h = 27.8 m/s → E = ½ · 1200 · 27.8² = 463,700 J

Twice the speed means four times the energy. Because the brakes have to turn that energy into heat while their force stays roughly the same, the braking distance is four times as long as well. That is the reason driving instructors teach a rule of thumb built on the square of the speed.

The roller coaster as a worked example

A roller coaster is the cleanest demonstration of the law there is. At the top of the first hill the car holds almost nothing but potential energy; at the bottom almost nothing but kinetic energy. For a drop of 40 metres, ignoring friction, the speed at the bottom follows directly from m · g · h = ½ · m · v². The mass cancels, leaving v = √(2 · 9.81 · 40) = 28.0 m/s, just over 100 km/h. Notice what is missing from that calculation: the weight of the car and the number of passengers. A full train and an empty one reach the bottom at the same speed. That is also why every following hill has to be lower than the one before — friction and air resistance take a share on the way, and no coaster can ever climb back to its starting height.
