Chloroplasts Explained Simply

Chloroplasts are the organelles in which photosynthesis takes place. They capture light and use it to build sugar, which makes them the only place in the whole food web where new chemical energy comes in. Everything else lives off it.

6 CO₂ + 6 H₂O + light → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6 O₂ carbon dioxide and water become glucose and oxygen
Key terms

Chlorophyll — the green pigment that absorbs light.
Thylakoids — flat membrane sacs; the chlorophyll sits here.
Grana — stacks of thylakoids.
Stroma — the fluid around them, where the Calvin cycle runs.
Light reaction — water is split, oxygen is released, ATP and NADPH are formed.
Calvin cycle — the light-independent reaction in which sugar is built from CO₂.

Why leaves are green

Chlorophyll absorbs mainly red and blue light. Green light is hardly taken up and is reflected instead, and this reflected green is exactly what we see. The colour of a leaf therefore shows the wavelength that the plant cannot use.

Why do leaves change colour in autumn?

Grandpa Theo

The plant breaks down the expensive chlorophyll and takes back the nitrogen. What remains are the yellow and red pigments that were hidden underneath before.

Mia

So they were there the whole time?

Grandpa Theo

Exactly. Nothing new is added. The green just disappears.

Chloroplasts were once free-living, too

Like mitochondria, chloroplasts have their own ring-shaped DNA, their own ribosomes and two membranes. They go back to cyanobacteria that were taken up by a cell: the same endosymbiont theory, only a second time in evolution.

The order matters. Mitochondria came first, in an ancestor of all cells with a nucleus. Only later did one line of these cells take up a cyanobacterium and keep it. That is why plants and algae have both mitochondria and chloroplasts, while animals and fungi have only mitochondria.

Common mistakes

“Plants do not breathe.” They break down sugar around the clock in their mitochondria. During the day, photosynthesis simply outweighs it.
Thinking the oxygen comes from the CO₂. It comes from the water, which is split in the light reaction.
Calling the Calvin cycle the “dark reaction”. It also runs in daylight; it just does not need light directly.
Chloroplasts in every plant cell. Root cells have none, because no light reaches them.

Practice task

A houseplant stands in a dark cellar for three weeks. Describe what happens to its leaves and justify it with the partial reactions of photosynthesis.

Show solution

Without light, the light reaction stops, so no ATP and no NADPH are formed. The Calvin cycle then lacks energy, and no more sugar is produced.
At first the plant lives off its starch reserves and breaks them down in the mitochondria. Chlorophyll is degraded because keeping it costs energy and brings no benefit, so the leaves turn yellow. In addition, the shoot stretches noticeably and forms only small leaves; this emergency response is called etiolation. Once the reserves are used up, the plant dies.

Light reaction and dark reaction

Photosynthesis in the chloroplast runs in two stages, and the two need very different conditions. They therefore also take place in different locations inside the same organelle.

The light reaction runs in the membranes of the thylakoids. Chlorophyll captures light energy and uses it to split water molecules. This releases the oxygen that the plant gives off, so it comes from the water and not from the carbon dioxide, which is a popular exam mistake. The freed electrons travel through a chain of protein complexes, and as in the mitochondrion, a proton gradient is built up, from which an ATP synthase makes ATP. In addition, the carrier molecule NADPH is produced at the end.

The dark reaction, also called the Calvin cycle, then runs in the stroma, the inner space between the thylakoids. It uses the ATP and NADPH from the light reaction to build carbon dioxide from the air into sugar. The name is misleading: it does not need darkness, it just does not need light, and because its fuel comes from the light reaction, it soon runs out at night anyway.

The slowest important protein in the world

The first step of the Calvin cycle is carried out by an enzyme called RuBisCO, and this enzyme is remarkable in two ways. First, it is probably the most abundant protein on Earth; in many plants it makes up around half of the soluble protein in the leaf. Second, it is quite slow and on top of that imprecise: it occasionally mistakes oxygen for carbon dioxide and builds in the wrong substance, which costs the plant energy. This happens especially often in heat, when the stomata are closed. That is exactly why workarounds have evolved in hot regions: maize and sugar cane concentrate carbon dioxide with a preliminary pathway before RuBisCO even gets to work. Cacti and pineapples go even further and collect the carbon dioxide at night, when they can open their stomata without losing much water.

Where the energy in your food really comes from

Follow any meal back far enough and you end up at a chloroplast. Bread comes from grain, which stored the sugar made in its leaves. Cheese and meat come from animals that ate plants. Even a fish that eats other fish depends in the end on algae, whose chloroplasts did the work. The sun's energy enters living nature at this single point, and every organism without chloroplasts, including you, lives on what they made. That is why the small green organelle is sometimes called the foundation of nearly all life on land and in water.

The oxygen in our atmosphere comes entirely from photosynthesis. About 2.4 billion years ago, cyanobacteria enriched the air with it, which for the living things of that time was the greatest poisoning in the history of the Earth.
