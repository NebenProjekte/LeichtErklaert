Viruses Explained Simply

Viruses are not living organisms. They have no metabolism, no cell and cannot multiply on their own. At their core they consist of genetic information inside a protein coat, and they work only if they find a foreign cell.

Genetic information + protein coat no cell, no metabolism, no reproduction of their own
Key terms

Capsid — the protein coat around the genetic material.
Envelope — in some viruses an additional membrane taken from the host cell. Soap destroys it.
Host cell — the cell that the virus uses to multiply.
Lytic cycle — the cell bursts and releases new viruses.
Lysogenic cycle — the viral genome rests in the genetic material of the cell and is copied along with it.
Host specificity — a virus fits only certain cell types, like a key and a lock.

The path of reproduction

Five steps
StepWhat happens
1. AttachmentThe virus docks onto a matching receptor of the cell
2. EntryThe genetic material gets inside the cell
3. TakeoverThe cell reads the viral information as if it were its own
4. AssemblyNew viruses are put together from the building blocks
5. ReleaseThe cell bursts or pinches the viruses off

Depending on the type of virus, a few hundred to several thousand new viruses can come out of a single infected cell. This explains why an infection can spread within hours.

💬 Mia asks Grandpa Theo

Mia

Why don't antibiotics help against the flu?

Grandpa Theo

Because they attack bacteria, either their cell wall or their ribosomes. A virus has neither. There is nothing to attack.

Mia

And why does washing your hands work so well?

Grandpa Theo

With enveloped viruses, soap dissolves the fatty envelope. Without the envelope, the virus can no longer dock.

Common mistakes

Calling viruses living organisms. Without a metabolism of their own, they do not meet the characteristics of life.
Antibiotics against viruses. Useless, and it encourages resistance.
Mixing up a virus with a bacterium. A bacterium is about a hundred times larger and a complete cell.
“A vaccine makes you immune at once.” The immune system needs one to three weeks to build up protection.

Practice task

Explain why there is a new vaccine against the flu every year, but not against measles.

Show solution

Flu viruses keep changing their surface proteins. Small mutations accumulate (antigenic drift), and when two virus types infect a cell at the same time, whole stretches of genes can be swapped (antigenic shift). The antibodies from the previous year then no longer fit, because they were tailored to the old surface.

The measles virus, by contrast, is genetically very stable and has only one serotype. Once formed, the antibodies fit for life. That is why two vaccinations in childhood are enough.

How a virus hijacks a cell

A virus cannot do anything by itself; it can only get other cells to build it. This process is the same for almost all viruses and can be broken down into five steps.

First comes docking. On the surface of the virus sit proteins that fit exactly to certain structures on the cell surface, like a key to a lock. Only where this lock is present can the virus attach at all. Then follows entry: either the virus envelope fuses with the cell membrane, or the cell takes in the virus by mistake, thinking it is food. In the third step, reprogramming, the genetic material is released. The cell does not distinguish between its own and foreign instructions and begins to read the viral genes. Next comes rebuilding: the cell produces viral building blocks at top speed, and these then assemble themselves into new viruses. At the end comes release: either the cell bursts, or the new viruses pinch off one by one and take a piece of the cell membrane with them as an envelope.

Right away or later: two strategies

Not every virus gets going at once. In the lytic pathway, production begins immediately, the cell dies within hours, and one falls ill quickly. In the lysogenic pathway, the virus instead builds its genetic material into that of the cell and lies dormant there, sometimes for years. The cell lives on and even passes the built-in virus to its daughter cells. Only when something flips the switch, such as stress, another infection or a weakened immune system, does the virus jump over to the lytic pathway. This is exactly what lies behind cold sores: the virus never disappears completely, it waits in nerve cells. And the same ability explains why so many old virus remnants sit in our own genetic material: they were built in and never removed.

From the key-and-lock principle of docking also follows host specificity. A virus that fits bird cells can usually do nothing with a human cell. If the surface protein changes through mutation so that it suddenly fits the human lock as well, however, a pathogen can cross the species barrier. Experts watch for exactly such jumps with flu viruses in particular.

Viruses in the lab and in medicine

Because viruses are so good at getting into cells, researchers have put them to work. Some viruses are used as delivery vehicles: their disease-causing genes are removed and replaced by a useful gene, which they then carry into the cells of a patient. Other viruses attack only bacteria; these are called bacteriophages, and they are being studied as a possible answer to bacteria that no longer respond to antibiotics. Vaccines, finally, train the immune system with harmless pieces or weakened forms of a virus, so that the real virus meets a prepared defence. In each of these cases the same basic fact is used: a virus is, in the end, a piece of information with a delivery mechanism.

About eight percent of the human genetic material comes from viruses that built themselves in millions of years ago. One of these genes is needed today for the formation of the placenta.
