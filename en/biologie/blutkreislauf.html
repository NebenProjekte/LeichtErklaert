The Circulatory System Explained Simply

The human circulatory system consists of two loops connected in series that share one pump. The heart first pushes blood through the lungs, where it picks up oxygen, and then through the rest of the body, where it gives that oxygen away. Once you have understood this order, you can read any diagram in a biology textbook.

body → right heart → lungs → left heart → body One complete circuit takes an adult at rest about one minute
Key terms

Artery — a vessel that leads away from the heart. It does not necessarily carry oxygen-rich blood.
Vein — a vessel that leads to the heart.
Capillary — a hair-thin vessel with a wall only one cell thick. This is where the actual exchange takes place.
Pulmonary circulation — the small loop, from the right half of the heart to the lungs and back.
Systemic circulation — the large loop, from the left half of the heart through the body and back.
Cardiac output — the volume of blood the heart pumps out per minute.

Two loops, one heart

A solid wall divides the heart into two halves that work at the same time but separately. The right half receives used, oxygen-poor blood from the body and sends it to the lungs, where it releases carbon dioxide and takes on oxygen. The refreshed blood returns to the left half, which pumps it around the whole body at a much higher pressure. That is why the left ventricle is far more muscular than the right: it works against roughly five times the resistance.

Right heart to the lungs, left heart to the body. Red marks oxygen-rich blood, blue oxygen-poor blood.

This is where most people trip over the vocabulary. Arteries and veins are not named after their oxygen content but after the direction of flow. The pulmonary artery therefore carries oxygen-poor blood because it leads away from the heart, and the pulmonary vein carries oxygen-rich blood because it leads towards it. This is the only place in the body where it works this way round, and it is exactly what exam questions ask about.

Where the work happens

The large vessels are only transport routes. The real purpose of the circulation is fulfilled in the capillaries. They are so narrow that red blood cells have to pass through them one behind the other, and their wall consists of a single layer of cells. Oxygen diffuses through this wall into the tissue, and carbon dioxide diffuses back into the blood. All the capillaries of one adult laid end to end would stretch for tens of thousands of kilometres and offer an exchange area the size of several tennis courts. This huge area is the reason why the exchange works despite the short contact time.

Worked example: cardiac output

Stroke volume at rest: about 70 ml
Heart rate at rest: about 70 beats per minute

CO = 70 ml · 70 /min = 4,900 ml/min ≈ 5 litres per minute

Under exertion: stroke volume 110 ml, rate 180 /min
CO = 110 · 180 = 19,800 ml/min ≈ 20 litres per minute

The body contains only about five to six litres of blood. At rest, then, roughly the entire blood volume is circulated once per minute, and under exertion four times as often.

Why veins need valves

By the time the blood has passed through the capillaries, almost all of the pressure is used up. In the veins of the legs it still has to travel upwards against gravity. The pressure of the pump no longer helps here; the muscle pump does. Every time the calf muscles tense, they squeeze the veins. So that the blood cannot slip back downwards, the veins contain pocket valves that open in one direction only. Anyone who stands motionless for a long time switches this pump off. Blood then pools in the legs and you feel dizzy.

💬 Mia asks Grandpa Theo

Mia

Why is the wall of the left ventricle thicker than the right one?

Grandpa Theo

Because it has to supply a much longer route. The right side only pumps to the lungs and back, the left side all the way to your toes. That takes about five times the pressure, and more muscle to produce it.

Mia

And why is the pulmonary artery called an artery when there is no oxygen in it?

Grandpa Theo

Because the name comes from the direction, not from the contents. Away from the heart is an artery, towards the heart is a vein. In the lungs the oxygen content flips, but the names do not.

Common mistakes

Equating "artery" with "oxygen-rich". In the pulmonary artery it is the other way round.
Drawing the two loops side by side. They run one after the other, and all the blood passes through both.
Thinking of the heart as one chamber. There are four: two atria and two ventricles, separated by valves and a dividing wall.
Expecting exchange in the large vessels. It takes place only in the capillaries.

Practice task

An athlete has a stroke volume of 120 ml and a resting pulse of 45 beats per minute. Calculate his cardiac output at rest and explain why trained people often have such a low resting pulse.

Show solution

CO = 120 ml · 45 /min = 5,400 ml/min = 5.4 litres per minute

That is exactly the amount an untrained person needs at rest, but the untrained person manages it with 70 ml per beat and about 77 beats. Endurance training enlarges the heart and makes it fill better, so the stroke volume rises. Since the demand at rest stays the same, the heart can beat less often. This is called athlete's bradycardia; it is a sign of good endurance fitness and does not need treatment.
