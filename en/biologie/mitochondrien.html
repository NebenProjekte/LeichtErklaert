Mitochondria Explained Simply

Mitochondria are the power plants of the cell. They convert the energy from food into a form the cell can actually work with: ATP. Without them, every muscle movement and every thought would come to a standstill.

C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6 O₂ → 6 CO₂ + 6 H₂O + energy Cellular respiration · one molecule of glucose yields up to 32 ATP
Key terms

ATP — adenosine triphosphate, the energy currency of the cell.
Cellular respiration — the breakdown of glucose with oxygen.
Matrix — the inner space of the mitochondrion.
Cristae — the folds of the inner membrane; they increase its surface area.
Respiratory chain — enzyme complexes in the inner membrane, where most of the ATP is made.
Endosymbiont theory — the explanation of where mitochondria come from.

Why they have two membranes

The outer membrane is smooth, the inner one is heavily folded. These folds, the cristae, increase the surface area many times over, and it is exactly on this surface that the respiratory chain sits. More surface means more ATP per unit of time. That is why the cristae are packed especially densely in heart muscle cells.

The endosymbiont theory

Mitochondria were once independent bacteria. A primitive cell took them in but did not digest them; both benefited and stayed together. Four observations support this: mitochondria have their own ring-shaped DNA, their own ribosomes of the bacterial type and two membranes, and they multiply by dividing, independently of cell division.

💬 Mia asks Grandpa Theo

Mia

If mitochondria used to be bacteria, are they still alive?

Grandpa Theo

Not on their own any more. Most of their genes moved into the cell nucleus long ago. Without the cell they would be lost.

Mia

Why do we inherit mitochondria only from our mother?

Grandpa Theo

Because the egg cell brings them along, and the sperm's mitochondria are broken down after fertilisation. That is why they can be used to trace the maternal line back.

Common mistakes

Mitochondria produce energy. They only convert it. Energy is never created.
Mixing them up with chloroplasts. Chloroplasts capture energy from light, mitochondria release it.
Only in animal cells. Plant cells have both.
One mitochondrion per cell. There are hundreds to thousands, depending on need.

Practice task

Name three pieces of evidence for the endosymbiont theory and explain why mature red blood cells have no mitochondria.

Show solution

Evidence: their own ring-shaped DNA as in bacteria, their own ribosomes of the prokaryotic type, a double membrane as a remnant of the uptake process, and independent multiplication by division. Three of these are enough.

Mature erythrocytes have broken down their nucleus and mitochondria. First, this makes room for haemoglobin; second, mitochondria of their own would use up part of the oxygen being transported. The cell covers its low energy needs through anaerobic glycolysis instead.

How ATP is made in the respiratory chain

It is quickly said that mitochondria make ATP. The way they do it is the more elegant part of the story, because the cell literally builds itself a hydroelectric power plant for the job.

In the heavily folded inner membrane sits a series of protein complexes, the respiratory chain. From the breakdown of food, energy-rich electrons arrive, bound to the carrier molecules NADH and FADH2. These electrons are passed from complex to complex and give up a little energy at each step. The complexes do not use this energy directly for ATP. Instead they use it to pump protons from the inner space of the mitochondrion into the gap between the two membranes. There the protons build up: many outside, few inside.

ATP synthase is a real turbine

The inner membrane is practically impermeable to protons, except at one kind of spot. There sits ATP synthase, and it really works mechanically: the returning stream of protons sets a ring-shaped part of the protein rotating, at roughly a hundred turns per second. With each third of a turn, the molecule presses one ADP and one phosphate together, and an ATP is ready. Roughly three to four protons have to pass through for a single ATP. At the end of the chain, oxygen takes up the spent electrons and, together with protons, becomes water. This is why oxygen is indispensable on this route, even though it takes no part in the energy gain itself. It only clears the end of the chain. If it is missing, everything backs up, the electrons have nowhere to go, and the whole chain stops.

Now the shape makes sense, too. The folds of the inner membrane, the cristae, increase the surface area many times over, and the more surface there is, the more chains and turbines fit in. Cells with a high constant demand therefore build especially many and especially densely folded mitochondria. A heart muscle cell consists of about a third mitochondria by volume, a skin cell only of a small fraction.

What happens before the respiratory chain

The electrons that reach the respiratory chain do not appear out of nowhere. Glucose is first split in the fluid of the cell into two smaller molecules, a step called glycolysis, which needs no oxygen and yields only a little ATP. The fragments then enter the mitochondrion, where they are broken down completely to carbon dioxide in a circular series of reactions known as the citric acid cycle. On the way, this cycle loads up the carriers NADH and FADH2 with electrons. The respiratory chain then turns these loaded carriers into the bulk of the ATP. Without oxygen, cells can only fall back on glycolysis, which is why a muscle that is pushed to its limit without enough oxygen tires so quickly.

A human turns over about as much ATP per day as he or she weighs, around 60 to 80 kilograms. This is possible only because each ATP molecule is reused thousands of times a day.
