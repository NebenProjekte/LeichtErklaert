Biology - Ecology

Ecosystems Explained Simply

An ecosystem is a community of all the living things in one place, together with their non-living surroundings - that is, soil, water, light and climate. All the parts are closely connected.

Producers → consumers → decomposers plants produce energy, animals eat them, and decomposers turn dead remains back into nutrients

Energy flows from plants through herbivores to carnivores, while decomposers return nutrients from dead material back into the cycle.

💬 Mia asks Grandpa Theo

Mia

Can an ecosystem also be very small, like a garden pond?

Grandpa Theo

Absolutely! Size does not matter - a garden pond with algae, frogs and insect larvae is just as much a real ecosystem as an entire rainforest. All that matters is that living things and environment together form a working whole.

Mia

What happens if a single animal disappears from such a system?

Grandpa Theo

That can ripple through the whole system, because everything is linked - this is called a chain reaction. If a predator disappears, for example, its prey can multiply unchecked and in turn overgraze the plants that other species live from.

Mia

What exactly do the decomposers in the picture do?

Grandpa Theo

Fungi, bacteria and soil animals such as earthworms break down dead plants and animals into their basic building blocks - and these end up as nutrients in the soil again, where new plants can take them up. Without them the whole cycle would collapse.

A chain reaction in real life

When wolves were wiped out in Yellowstone, deer multiplied unchecked and stripped young trees bare - only the reintroduction of the wolves brought the whole system back into balance.

Ecosystems can adapt to small changes, but interventions that are too large or too fast - such as wiping out a species - often throw them permanently out of balance.

Key terms

Biotope — the habitat with soil, water and climate.
Biocoenosis — the community of all species living in it.
Ecosystem — biotope and biocoenosis together.
Trophic level — the position in the food web: producer, consumer, decomposer.
Ecological niche — the sum of a species' requirements, not its location.
Succession — the orderly sequence of communities up to the final stage.

At every trophic level, about 90 percent of the energy is lost as heat. That is why food chains stay short, and why there are many herbivores and few top predators. Carbon, nitrogen and phosphorus, on the other hand, are made available again by the decomposers and run through the system once more.

From food web to keystone species

In nature, food chains are rarely as simple as in the diagram. Almost every animal eats several species and is eaten by several, so a food web arises. This makes an ecosystem more stable: if one prey species fails, the predators switch to another. Even so, there are species whose loss has a disproportionate effect. They are called keystone species. A well-known example is the sea otter on the Pacific coast. It eats sea urchins. Without the otters, the sea urchins multiply strongly and graze down the kelp forests in which many other species find shelter and food. The loss of a single predator then changes an entire stretch of coast.

How bare ground becomes forest

After a glacier retreats, a flood or a fire, colonisation starts again from scratch. First come pioneer species such as lichens, mosses and grasses, which get by with few nutrients. Over time they build up humus. Shrubs and light-loving trees such as birch and pine settle on it. Under their canopy, shade-tolerant species sprout and gradually push them out. In central Europe this sequence usually ends in a beech forest. This development is called succession. Each stage changes the soil and the microclimate so that the next stage finds better conditions.

Common mistakes

Equating biotope and ecosystem. The biotope is only the space.
Understanding the niche as a place. It describes the requirements, not the address - two species in the same place can occupy different niches.
Drawing energy as a cycle. Only matter cycles.
Leaving out the decomposers. Without them the cycle of matter stops.

Practice task

A lake is over-fertilised by fertiliser from agriculture. Describe the chain of consequences up to the fish kill and name the technical term.

Show solution

The technical term is eutrophication.
Nitrate and phosphate act as fertiliser, and algae multiply explosively. The layer of algae at the surface takes the light away from the deeper water, where the aquatic plants die. Dying algae and plants sink to the bottom, where the decomposers break them down - using up a great deal of oxygen in the process. The deep water becomes low in oxygen, and fish suffocate.
In the final stage, anaerobic bacteria carry on working and produce foul gases such as hydrogen sulphide. The lake "tips over". It is remarkable that at the start there is a nutrient surplus and at the end an oxygen shortage.
