Rivers as Ecosystems Explained Simply

A river is not a uniform ecosystem. It changes greatly on its way from the source to the mouth, and with it change the temperature, the current and the living things found there.

Source → upper course → middle course → lower course → mouth from cold, fast and rich in oxygen to warm, slow and rich in nutrients

In the upper course the water is cold and fast, in the lower course it is wider, warmer and slower - completely different conditions on one and the same river.

💬 Mia asks Grandpa Theo

Mia

Why do trout mostly live further up the river?

Grandpa Theo

Because cold, fast-flowing water holds more dissolved oxygen than warm, slow water - and trout need a particularly large amount of it. In the upper course they find exactly these conditions.

Mia

And why does a river keep getting wider towards the mouth?

Grandpa Theo

On the way, many small streams and tributaries flow in, and the amount of water keeps growing. At the same time the slope becomes gentler, so the water flows more slowly and deposits the mud and sand it has been carrying.

Mia

Why is the mouth so often such a special habitat?

Grandpa Theo

That is where fresh water from the river meets salt water from the sea and mixes with it - this is called brackish water. Only a few, especially adaptable species can cope with the changing salt content, but in return many young fish and birds find plenty of food there.

Travelers between worlds

Salmon are born in fresh water, spend most of their lives in the sea, and then swim back against the current to exactly the river where they hatched, in order to spawn there themselves.

Because conditions change so much along a river, a single river is often home to an astonishing variety of differently adapted species.

Key terms

Source, upper course, middle course, lower course, mouth — the sections of a river.
Erosion — wearing away by flowing water.
Sedimentation — deposition when the current slows down.
Meander — a loop of the river in the middle and lower course.
Cut bank — the outside of a bend, fast current, material is worn away.
Slip-off slope — the inside of a bend, slow current, material is deposited.

Why rivers form loops

In a bend, the water flows faster on the outside than on the inside. On the outside it wears material away, on the inside it deposits some. This makes the bend more and more pronounced over time - until the loop cuts itself off and is left behind as an oxbow lake. The river thus keeps shifting its bed without anyone helping it along.

Who lives where: the fish zones

Because temperature, current and oxygen content change along the river, the fish fauna also follows a fixed order. The cold, fast upper course is the trout zone. Below it comes the grayling zone, where the water is somewhat warmer and calmer. In the middle course, the barbel zone is home to fish that can also cope with less oxygen and a gravelly bottom. In the slow, warm lower course, the bream zone, you find bream, carp and tench, which search for food on the bottom of the murky water. The names come from the typical species in each zone. The boundaries between the zones are gradual and depend on the size and altitude of the river.

Small animals as measuring instruments

You can tell how clean a river is from its inhabitants. The larvae of stoneflies and mayflies need a lot of oxygen and occur only in clean water. Red midge larvae and sludge worms, by contrast, tolerate even low-oxygen, heavily polluted water. From the mix of species, experts determine the water quality and place the river in a quality class from I (unpolluted) to IV (excessively polluted). Such species are called indicator species.

A river in a natural state also partly cleans itself: bacteria and tiny organisms on stones, plants and in the gravel break down organic waste. This self-purification has limits. If too much is discharged, the decomposers use up the dissolved oxygen, and the sensitive species disappear first.

Common mistakes

Mixing up cut bank and slip-off slope. The cut bank is on the outside and steep, the slip-off slope on the inside and flat.
"A river transports the same amount everywhere." Transport capacity grows with a high power of the flow velocity - small changes have a strong effect.
Confusing delta and estuary. A delta forms without strong tides, an estuary with them.
Seeing straightening as flood protection. It speeds up the water and makes the situation worse downstream.

Practice task

Explain why straightening a river increases the flood risk downstream, and name two countermeasures.

Show solution

A straightened river is shorter and therefore has a steeper slope. The water runs off faster and the travel time drops. At the same time, the floodplains that used to absorb large amounts of water and release it slowly are missing. The flood wave therefore arrives further down faster and higher - and may coincide with the wave of a tributary.

Countermeasures: renaturation, that is, restoring meanders and removing bank reinforcements, and recovering flood plains by moving dikes back. Both slow down the runoff and create retention. In addition, unsealing surfaces in the catchment area helps, because less water runs off over the surface.
