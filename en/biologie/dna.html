Biology - Genetics

DNA Explained Simply

DNA is the blueprint of every living thing. It stores information not in words but in a sequence of four building blocks, and this sequence can be copied, passed on and read.

A – T  ·  G – C Adenine pairs with thymine, guanine with cytosine · always, without exception
Key terms

Nucleotide — a building block made of a sugar, a phosphate and a base.
Base — adenine, thymine, guanine or cytosine.
Double helix — two strands wound around each other.
Complementarity — each strand contains the complete information about the other.
Gene — a section that carries the instructions for building a protein.
Replication — doubling the DNA before every cell division.

Why the pairing is so important

Because A always fits with T and G always fits with C, one strand is enough to rebuild the other. This is exactly what copying relies on: the double helix is opened like a zipper, and the matching counterpart attaches to each half strand. One molecule becomes two identical ones.

Common mistakes

Treating DNA and chromosome as the same thing. A chromosome is tightly packed DNA, visible only during division.
Uracil in DNA. U occurs only in RNA, where it replaces thymine.
“Every gene is one trait.” Most traits arise from many genes working together and from the environment.
Pairing the bases the wrong way round. A with T and G with C, never A with G.

Practice task

A DNA strand reads 5'-ATG CGA TTC-3'. Give the complementary strand and explain why replication is called “semiconservative”.

Show solution

Complementary: 3'-TAC GCT AAG-5'
Each base is replaced by its fixed partner, and the opposite strand runs in the opposite direction.

Semiconservative means that after doubling, each of the two new molecules consists of one old strand and one newly built strand. Half is therefore preserved. Meselson and Stahl showed this in 1958 by labelling DNA with heavy nitrogen and measuring its density after each division. The intermediate density after the first round ruled out all the other models.

From gene to protein

DNA stores information, but it is never read directly on the spot. The path from a gene to a finished protein runs in two steps, with a transcript in between.

The first step is called transcription. An enzyme opens the double helix at the location of the gene in question and copies one of the two strands. The result is mRNA, a single-stranded copy that can leave the cell nucleus. That is the real point of this detour: the DNA itself stays safely in the nucleus, and only the working copy is sent out. In the copy, the base uracil stands in place of thymine; otherwise the same pairing rules apply.

The second step is called translation and takes place at the ribosome. There the mRNA is read in steps of three. Every three bases form a codon and stand for exactly one amino acid. Matching transport molecules bring the respective amino acid, the ribosome attaches it to the growing chain, and from this chain the finished protein finally folds.

Why three bases and not two

That groups of three are read is no coincidence, but the smallest solution that works. There are four bases and twenty amino acids to be encoded. With groups of two there would be only 4 · 4 = 16 possibilities, which is too few. With groups of three there are 4 · 4 · 4 = 64 codons, more than enough. The surplus is not wasted: three codons serve as a stop signal and end the chain, and one also serves as the start signal. The rest are distributed so that most amino acids are encoded by several different codons. This makes the code astonishingly tolerant of errors: if a mutation swaps the third base of a codon, the same amino acid very often comes out anyway, and the protein stays unchanged. The code is also almost identical in bacteria, plants and humans. It was apparently fixed very early and has hardly changed since, and that is exactly why a human gene can be built into a bacterium, which then produces a human protein such as insulin.

Worked example: reading a short message

To see transcription and translation in action, take the DNA segment 3'-TAC GCT AAG-5'. The mRNA copy has the complementary bases, with U instead of T, so it reads 5'-AUG CGA UUC-3'. The ribosome reads it codon by codon: AUG is the start signal and also stands for the amino acid methionine, CGA stands for arginine, and UUC for phenylalanine. The short chain produced is therefore methionine, arginine, phenylalanine. If a mutation changed the last base of the second codon from A to G, CGG would still stand for arginine and nothing would change. If it changed the first base of that codon from C to U, UGA would appear, which is a stop signal, and the chain would end after a single amino acid. A tiny change in the letters can therefore have no effect at all, or a very large one, depending on where it hits.

If you laid out the DNA of all the cells of one person end to end, it would come to about 150 billion kilometres, roughly a thousand times the distance from the Earth to the Sun.
