The Forest Ecosystem Explained Simply

A forest is not a heap of trees but an ecosystem: a web of living things and their environment, in which energy flows through and materials run in a cycle. Once you keep these two apart, you understand the rest at once.

Energy flows through · materials run in a cycle the sun keeps supplying energy, carbon and nitrogen are used again and again
Key terms

Producers — plants; they build organic substances from light.
Consumers — animals; they eat. First order = plant eaters, second order = meat eaters.
Decomposers — fungi, bacteria and soil animals; they break dead material down into minerals.
Biotope — the habitat with its soil, climate and water.
Biocoenosis — the community of all species living in it.
Stratification — the layering from the moss layer up to the tree crowns.

The layering is no accident but an answer to light. On the forest floor, often less than two percent of the sunlight arrives. That is why wood anemones flower in March, before the beeches come into leaf.

Why the decomposers are the most important

Without decomposers, the leaves would pile up year after year and the nutrients would stay locked inside them. Earthworms break them into pieces, and fungi and bacteria mineralise them. Only then are nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium available to the roots again. One hectare of forest soil contains several tonnes of living things, more biomass than all the forest animals above it put together.

💬 Mia asks Grandpa Theo

Mia

Why is a spruce plantation worse than a mixed forest?

Grandpa Theo

Because all the trees are the same age and equally vulnerable. When the bark beetle comes, it hits every one. In a mixed forest it finds only some of them.

Mia

And what about the needles?

Grandpa Theo

They rot slowly and make the soil more acidic. Beech leaves are gone in a year, spruce needles take up to seven.

Common mistakes

A food chain instead of a food web. Almost every species eats several others; chains are only a simplification.
Forgetting the decomposers. Without them, the cycle of materials collapses.
Drawing energy as a cycle. Energy flows through and is lost as heat; only materials circulate.
Treating biotope and biocoenosis as the same thing. The biotope is the space, the biocoenosis is the community. Together they make the ecosystem.

Practice task

Of the solar energy that a plant captures, only about 10% reaches the next trophic level. Explain where the rest goes, and use this to explain why food chains rarely have more than four links.

Show solution

Most of it is used up in respiration and given off as heat, because every living thing needs energy for movement, growth and maintenance. Further portions are contained in parts that cannot be used, such as bones or cellulose, and pass with the droppings to the decomposers.

At 10% per level, of 10,000 energy units in the plants, 1,000 remain with the plant eater, 100 with the first meat eater and 10 with the second. From the fifth level on, the energy is no longer enough to feed a population. That is why there are many mice, few foxes, and no animals that live on wolves.

What a forest does for us

A forest produces more than timber. It provides a series of services that one notices only when they fail, and which can be put into surprisingly concrete numbers.

The best known is the binding of carbon. One hectare of Central European forest takes up on average a few tonnes of carbon dioxide a year and stores the carbon in wood, roots and forest soil. What matters here is that an old forest stores a lot but grows slowly, while a young forest stores little but grows fast. Only both together make a stable store. Almost as important is water management. The canopy holds back rain, the litter layer acts like a sponge, and the rooted soil lets water seep away slowly instead of running off over the surface. That is why forest on a slope protects against erosion, and in a catchment area against flood peaks. Incidentally, the seeping water is filtered, and a considerable share of German drinking water comes from forested areas.

The climate under the trees

Anyone who steps from a field into a forest in summer feels the difference at once, and it is measurable: on hot days the temperature in a beech forest is often 4 to 8 degrees below that in the open. Two processes work together. First, the canopy shades the ground; depending on the tree species, only a few percent of the light arrives below, and light that does not arrive does not heat either. Second, the trees evaporate large amounts of water through their leaves, and every evaporation cools. A full-grown beech gives off several hundred litres of water on a warm summer day. In addition, the forest slows the wind and keeps the humidity high. Exactly this combination makes the forest edge a habitat of its own: there the moist, shady inner climate meets the dry, bright outer climate, and that is why more species live at the forest edge than in the interior behind it.

These services also explain why converting spruce plantations into mixed forests is pursued at such expense. A species-rich forest with trees of different ages absorbs disturbances that a uniform planting would lose within a single season.

A year on the forest floor

The seasons show how the layers and the cycle of materials fit together. In early spring, before the trees have leaves, light still reaches the ground and the herb layer flowers in a rush: wood anemones and other early bloomers use these few weeks to build up reserves in their roots and bulbs. When the beeches come into leaf, the forest floor falls into shade and these plants die back above ground. In summer the canopy does the work, binding carbon and cooling the air. In autumn the leaves fall, and the decomposers start their work: earthworms pull leaves into the soil, fungi and bacteria break them down, and by the following spring the nutrients are available to the roots again. The same atoms of nitrogen and carbon may go round this loop many times, while the energy, in contrast, arrives from the sun every day and leaves as heat.

Beeches and fungi live in symbiosis: the fungal network envelops the fine roots, delivers water and minerals and receives sugar in return. Through this network, trees even exchange nutrients with each other.
