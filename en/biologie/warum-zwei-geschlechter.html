Biology - Evolutionary Biology

Why There Are Biologically Two Sexes

In biology, the sex of an organism is defined not by appearance or behaviour but by the type of reproductive cells (gametes) it produces. And in sexual reproduction there are only two types of these.

Large, immobile gametes + small, mobile gametes Egg cells (female) are large and rich in nutrients, sperm cells (male) are small and mobile - this two-way split is called anisogamy

A single egg cell is thousands of times larger than a single sperm cell - this difference in size is the biological basis of the sexes.

💬 Mia asks Grandpa Theo

Mia

Why exactly two sexes and not three or four? More variety in reproduction doesn't sound bad at first.

Grandpa Theo

Good question - biologists have worked out exactly this with evolutionary models. At the very beginning of evolution, all gametes were the same size. But there were two competing strategies for success: either produce many tiny, mobile gametes that reach as many other gametes as possible - or a few large gametes with plenty of nutrient reserves that give the offspring a good start.

Mia

And why didn't a medium size win out as a compromise?

Grandpa Theo

Because medium-sized gametes lose on both counts: they are too large and slow to reach many partners, but too small to give the offspring enough nutrients. The two extremes - tiny and mobile, or huge and rich in nutrients - had the evolutionary advantage, so in the end exactly these two strategies won out and stayed stable. This holds for practically all plants, animals and fungi with sexual reproduction.

Mia

Is that the same for us humans as the X and Y chromosomes we have already talked about?

Grandpa Theo

An important distinction: X and Y are only the mechanism that determines in humans which gamete type is produced - not the sex itself. Other species use other mechanisms: birds and butterflies have a reversed system (ZW), in some reptiles the temperature in the egg decides, and clownfish can even change their role during their lifetime. The mechanism differs greatly - but the result is almost everywhere the same: egg cell or sperm cell, nothing in between.

Biological variation within the system

Even though there are only two gamete types, the development of an individual body can take many paths: in intersex people, rare variations in chromosomes, hormones or development lead to body features that do not clearly fit the usual pattern. This does not contradict the two-way split of gamete types, but it shows that physical development can vary along this system.

How sex is determined

That there are two gamete types says nothing about how it is decided in an individual organism which type it produces. Nature takes surprisingly different routes here, and the route via chromosomes is only one of them.

In humans and most mammals, the XY system decides: two X chromosomes lead to the production of egg cells, one X and one Y to the production of sperm cells. In birds and butterflies it works exactly the other way round; there the system is called ZW - here it is the females that carry two different sex chromosomes. Many reptiles have no sex chromosomes at all. In crocodiles and many turtles, it is instead the incubation temperature in the nest that decides which gametes the animal will later produce - a difference of a few degrees reverses the outcome. In some ants and bees, in turn, an unfertilised egg produces an animal with a single set of chromosomes, and a fertilised one an animal with a double set.

Changing over the course of life

Some species are not committed to one gamete type for life. Many damselfish and wrasses start out producing egg cells and later switch to producing sperm cells when there is no large male left in the group - in clownfish it is the other way round. Earthworms and many snails even produce both gamete types at the same time and are therefore called hermaphrodites. When they mate, the two animals fertilise each other. That is practical if you are slow and rarely meet a partner. None of these cases changes the basic question from the first part: even in a hermaphrodite there are exactly two kinds of sex cells, large immobile ones and small mobile ones. The only thing that differs is how many individuals produce which type, and whether they do it at the same time or one after the other.

For biology this is an important point: the two-way split lies in the sex cells, not in the blueprints for whole bodies. That is why the definition by gamete type works right across the animal and plant kingdoms, while any definition by appearance, chromosomes or behaviour fails again as soon as you reach the next animal group.

This explanation deliberately limits itself to the biological definition by gamete types as used in evolutionary biology - a narrow scientific concept that does not cover social or personal questions about gender.
