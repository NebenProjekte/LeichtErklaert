Bacteria Explained Simply

Bacteria are single-celled organisms without a nucleus. Their genetic information floats freely inside the cell instead of sitting in its own envelope. This one difference separates them from all animals, plants and fungi, and it is the reason antibiotics can hit bacteria without destroying our own cells.

Prokaryote = cell without a nucleus Bacteria and archaea · all other living things are eukaryotes
Key terms

Prokaryote — a cell with no true nucleus and no membrane-bound organelles.
Nucleoid — the region where the ring-shaped bacterial chromosome lies.
Plasmid — a small extra ring of DNA, often carrying resistance genes. Plasmids can be passed between bacteria.
Cell wall — a casing made of murein (peptidoglycan), the target of many antibiotics.
Binary fission — reproduction by dividing into two identical cells.
Gram stain — a staining test that sorts bacteria into Gram-positive and Gram-negative according to the structure of their cell wall.

How fast they multiply

Under good conditions, Escherichia coli divides every 20 minutes.

after 1 hour: 2³ = 8
after 5 hours: 215 = 32,768
after 10 hours: 230 ≈ 1.07 billion

These numbers explain why spoiled food seems to turn so suddenly and why a wound infection can become dangerous within hours. In reality, lack of space, lack of nutrients and the bacteria's own waste products slow the growth down. Otherwise the Earth would be covered by a layer of bacteria within a few days.

Doubling is the key idea here. A quantity that doubles at regular intervals grows exponentially: the first doublings look harmless (1, 2, 4, 8), but each step adds as many new cells as all the previous steps combined. That is why an infection can seem insignificant in the morning and be serious by evening.

💬 Mia asks Grandpa Theo

Mia

Aren't all bacteria dangerous?

Grandpa Theo

Quite the opposite. Trillions of them live in your gut, where they help you digest food and produce vitamin K. The ones that cause disease are a minority.

Mia

And why do antibiotics sometimes stop working?

Grandpa Theo

Because a few individual bacteria happen to be resistant. They survive, they multiply, and they even hand their plasmids on to other species.

Why antibiotics do not harm us

Many antibiotics attack the murein cell wall or the bacteria's own ribosomes, which differ from ours. Human cells have no murein wall, so the drug simply finds no target there. This is also why antibiotics do not work against viruses: viruses have neither a cell wall nor ribosomes of their own.

The bacterial cell wall is worth a closer look, because it explains a lot. Inside a bacterium the pressure is higher than outside, and without a sturdy wall the cell would swell and burst. An antibiotic that blocks the building of the wall therefore does not just slow the bacterium down: each time the cell grows or divides, the wall has weak spots, the cell takes in water and bursts. Our own cells never depend on such a wall, so they are left alone.

Common mistakes

Confusing bacteria with viruses. Viruses are not cells and can only multiply inside other cells.
“Bacteria have no DNA.” They do have DNA, just no nucleus around it.
Taking antibiotics for a cold. Colds are mostly viral, so the drug does no good and encourages resistance.
Reading resistance as a person getting used to the drug. The person does not become resistant, the bacterium does.

Practice task

Explain why an antibiotic that blocks cell wall synthesis does not attack human cells. Also explain why stopping a course of antibiotics after three days instead of seven promotes resistance.

Show solution

Human cells have no cell wall made of murein, only a cell membrane. The drug blocks an enzyme that does not exist in the human body, so the point of attack is simply missing.

If treatment stops too early, the most sensitive bacteria have already been killed, but the hardier ones are still there. They keep multiplying undisturbed, and the next generation descends entirely from the tougher cells. That is exactly the selection one wanted to avoid.

How bacteria adapt

Bacteria do not only multiply quickly, they also change quickly. They have two ways of doing this, and the second one is the truly worrying one.

The first way is mutation. Every time a cell divides, rare mistakes creep in when the DNA is copied. Because a culture divides every 20 minutes under good conditions, billions of copying events add up in a single day, and with them plenty of random variants. Most of these are useless or harmful, but as soon as an antibiotic is added, exactly those few whose random variant happens to protect them survive. They keep multiplying, and after a short time the whole culture consists of their descendants. This is evolution in fast motion, observable within a single school lesson.

Bacteria swap genes directly

The second way has no counterpart in humans or animals: bacteria pass genetic information on not only to their offspring, but also sideways to other bacteria, even ones of a completely different species. Many carry, besides their main ring of DNA, small additional rings called plasmids. Resistance genes are often located on exactly these plasmids. Two bacteria can form a bridge and push a plasmid across; this takes minutes. A gut bacterium that has acquired resistance can in this way hand it on to a disease-causing bacterium that happens to live in the same gut. That is why resistance spreads much faster than the plain reproduction rate would explain, and why it makes sense to use antibiotics only when they are really needed: every unnecessary use is one more round of selection in favour of the resistant ones.

Bacteria also survive hard times better than most cells. Some species form spores when nutrients run out: they dry out their interior, pack their genetic material into a tough shell and shut down all metabolism. In this state they survive boiling, drying out and decades of waiting. When water and food return, they germinate again. This is exactly why brief heating is not enough when food is preserved.

Bacteria in everyday life

Most bacteria are harmless to us, and many are useful. Yoghurt, cheese and sauerkraut owe their taste and their shelf life to lactic acid bacteria, which turn sugar into lactic acid and so make the food too sour for its spoilers. Sewage treatment plants rely on bacteria to break down waste. In the soil, bacteria release nutrients from dead material, and some of them convert nitrogen from the air into a form that plants can use. Seen this way, the question is not how to get rid of bacteria, but how to live alongside the right ones and protect ourselves from the few harmful ones.

The human body contains roughly as many bacterial cells as body cells. Together they weigh about 200 grams, making them an organ of their own that you cannot see.
