Cellular Respiration Explained Simply

Cellular respiration is the process your cells use to extract usable energy from nutrients. It happens in several steps - first outside, then inside the mitochondria - and is basically the opposite of photosynthesis in plants.

C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6 O₂ → 6 CO₂ + 6 H₂O + ~30 ATP glucose and oxygen are converted into carbon dioxide, water, and usable energy in the form of ATP

Glycolysis happens freely in the cytoplasm and only supplies a little energy - the bulk of the ATP is produced afterward, inside the mitochondrion.

💬 Mia asks Grandpa Theo

Mia

Wait - cellular respiration means you're breathing with your cells instead of your lungs?

Grandpa Theo

The two are related, but not the same. Your lungs bring oxygen into your blood and carry carbon dioxide out - that's "breathing". Cellular respiration is the chemical process inside every single cell that uses this oxygen to generate energy.

Mia

What actually happens in those three steps - glycolysis, the citric acid cycle, the electron transport chain?

Grandpa Theo

Glycolysis breaks sugar down into smaller building blocks in the cytoplasm and already supplies a little energy - but without oxygen yet. Those building blocks then move into the mitochondrion: the citric acid cycle breaks them down further and supplies electrons, which the electron transport chain then uses - consuming oxygen - to produce most of the ATP. Only this last step actually needs oxygen.

Mia

Do plants do this too, even though they photosynthesize?

Grandpa Theo

Yes, absolutely! Photosynthesis produces the sugar the plant uses as its energy store - but to actually turn that sugar into usable energy, plant cells need cellular respiration too, just like animals and fungi. So during the day, plants run both processes at the same time.

Mia

So why do we actually breathe faster when we exercise?

Grandpa Theo

Because your muscle cells suddenly need much more energy and so ramp up cellular respiration. That takes more oxygen and produces more carbon dioxide - your body responds with faster breathing and a faster heartbeat to keep the supply coming.

Even without oxygen

When your muscles briefly run short on oxygen, like during a sprint, cells switch to an oxygen-free emergency mode: they stop after glycolysis and turn the pyruvate into lactic acid instead. This so-called lactic acid fermentation supplies far less ATP than full cellular respiration, but it works without oxygen and causes that familiar burning feeling in your muscles.

Photosynthesis and cellular respiration complement each other perfectly: one consumes CO₂ and produces oxygen, the other consumes oxygen and produces CO₂ - together they help keep the atmosphere's cycle in balance.
