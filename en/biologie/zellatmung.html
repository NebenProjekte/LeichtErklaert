Cellular Respiration Explained Simply

Written by Viktor Jonas · Reading time about 5 minutes

Cellular respiration is the process by which your cells obtain usable energy from nutrients. It takes place in several steps - first outside, then inside the mitochondria - and is basically the opposite of photosynthesis in plants.

C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6 O₂ → 6 CO₂ + 6 H₂O + ~30 ATP glucose and oxygen are converted into carbon dioxide, water and usable energy in the form of ATP

Glycolysis takes place freely in the cytoplasm and supplies only little energy - the lion's share of the ATP is produced afterwards, in the mitochondrion.

💬 Mia asks Grandpa Theo

Mia

Wait a moment - cellular respiration means you breathe with your cells and not with your lungs?

Grandpa Theo

The two belong together, but they are not the same thing. Your lungs bring oxygen into your blood and carry carbon dioxide out - that is "lung breathing". Cellular respiration is the chemical process that uses this oxygen in every single cell to produce energy.

Mia

What exactly happens in these three steps - glycolysis, the citric acid cycle and the electron transport chain?

Grandpa Theo

Glycolysis breaks the sugar down into smaller building blocks in the cytoplasm and already yields a little energy - but still without oxygen. These building blocks then move into the mitochondrion: in the citric acid cycle they are broken down further and deliver electrons, which then produce most of the ATP in the electron transport chain - using up oxygen. Only this last step really needs oxygen.

Mia

Do plants do this too, even though they carry out photosynthesis?

Grandpa Theo

Yes, absolutely! Photosynthesis produces the sugar that the plant uses as an energy store - but to actually turn this sugar into usable energy, plant cells also need cellular respiration, just like animals and fungi. During the day, both processes therefore run at the same time in plants.

Mia

Why do we breathe faster when we exercise, then?

Grandpa Theo

Because your muscle cells then need much more energy and therefore carry out much more cellular respiration. For that they need more oxygen and produce more carbon dioxide - your body responds with faster breathing and a faster heartbeat to deliver the supply.

Even without oxygen

When your muscles briefly run out of oxygen, for example in a sprint, cells switch to an oxygen-free emergency variant: they stop at glycolysis and convert the pyruvate into lactic acid instead. This so-called lactic acid fermentation yields much less ATP than full cellular respiration, but it works without oxygen and causes the familiar burning feeling in the muscles.

Photosynthesis and cellular respiration complement each other perfectly: one consumes CO₂ and produces oxygen, the other consumes oxygen and produces CO₂ - together they keep the cycle of the atmosphere in balance.

The balance sheet in numbers

How much does cellular respiration actually yield? From a single glucose molecule, a cell gains about 30 to 32 molecules of ATP under good conditions. The range arises because part of the energy is lost in transport between the cytoplasm and the mitochondrion, and this does not happen equally in every tissue. It is interesting how unevenly this yield is distributed: glycolysis supplies only 2 ATP net, and the citric acid cycle also 2. The remaining 26 to 28 or so are produced only in the last step, in the electron transport chain.

The reason is that the first two steps are not primarily about making ATP at all. They collect electrons and hand them over to two transport molecules, NADH and FADH2. These two bring their cargo to the inner mitochondrial membrane, and there the real trick begins.

The cell builds a hydroelectric power station

In the inner membrane sits a chain of protein complexes. The electrons from NADH and FADH2 are passed from one complex to the next and give off energy bit by bit as they go. The complexes use this energy to pump protons from the inside of the mitochondrion to the outside. This creates an imbalance: many protons outside, few inside. Just like water behind a dam, the protons press back - and the membrane lets them through at only one place, namely through ATP synthase. This protein really does work like a turbine: the flow of protons turns part of the molecule, and with every turn ADP is joined with a phosphate to make ATP. At the end of the chain, oxygen takes up the used electrons and becomes water. That is exactly what you breathe for - oxygen stands at the very end of the chain and keeps it moving. If it is missing, everything backs up, and the cell has to switch to fermentation.

This design also explains why the inner membrane is so heavily folded. The more surface area there is, the more chains and turbines fit in. Heart muscle cells, which never take a break, are therefore packed with mitochondria more densely than almost any other tissue in the body.

The three stages at a glance

Where each stage happens, what goes in, what comes out and how much ATP is left. The split is what surprises most: the first two stages barely make any ATP directly — their job is collecting electrons.

Glycolysis works without oxygen; the other two stages need it. That is why a muscle short of oxygen is left with glycolysis alone — and with a far smaller yield.

Four mix-ups that cost marks

This is where most marks are lost in exams.

Cellular respiration is not breathing
The lungs exchange gases with the air. Cellular respiration is a chemical reaction inside every single cell. The two are connected but not the same: a plant carries out cellular respiration and has no lungs.
38 ATP is the old figure
Many books still give 36 or 38 ATP per glucose. Today the figure is 30 to 32, because moving NADH from the cytoplasm into the mitochondrion costs energy too. If your book says 38, quote the number and explain why less is counted today.
Plants respire as well
Photosynthesis makes sugar, cellular respiration turns it into usable energy. A plant does both — at night, only the second.
Fermentation is not a stage of cellular respiration
When oxygen runs short, the cell stops at glycolysis and turns the pyruvate into lactate. That is an alternative route yielding only 2 ATP, not a fourth step of the process.

Practice: five questions, instant feedback

Click an answer. You see straight away whether it is right — and why.

  1. Where does glycolysis take place?

    In the cytoplasm, and without oxygen. That is why even a cell with no working mitochondria can still gain some energy.

  2. Which stage supplies most of the ATP?

    The transport chain, with 26 to 28 of the 30 to 32 ATP. The first two stages give 2 ATP each; their real job is collecting electrons.

  3. What is the role of oxygen in cellular respiration?

    Oxygen is the final electron acceptor. Without it the chain backs up, NADH is never emptied, and the first two stages come to a halt as well.

  4. How much ATP does one glucose molecule yield by today's reckoning?

    30 to 32. The figure 38 comes from older books, which did not subtract the cost of moving NADH from the cytoplasm into the mitochondrion.

  5. What happens in a muscle when oxygen runs short during a brief effort?

    That is lactic acid fermentation: only 2 ATP per glucose, but available at once and without oxygen. Which is why it only carries you through very short efforts.

Exam questions with model answers

Try it yourself first, then open the answer.

Describe the three stages of cellular respiration, giving location, starting materials, products and ATP yield.

Glycolysis, in the cytoplasm: one glucose is split into two molecules of pyruvate. Two ATP are used and four are made, so the net gain is 2 ATP, plus 2 NADH. No oxygen is needed.

Citric acid cycle, in the mitochondrial matrix: the pyruvate is fully oxidised and CO₂ is released. The direct yield is again 2 ATP per glucose, but many carrier molecules are loaded: 6 NADH and 2 FADH₂.

Electron transport chain, at the inner membrane: the electrons from NADH and FADH₂ pass through the protein complexes, protons are pumped into the intermembrane space, and their return through ATP synthase delivers 26 to 28 ATP. At the end oxygen takes up the electrons and water is formed.

Explain why cellular respiration almost stops without oxygen, even though oxygen is only needed in the last stage.

Oxygen is the final electron acceptor. If it is missing, the transport chain fills up and accepts no further electrons.

The result is a backlog: NADH and FADH₂ are never unloaded and stay in their reduced form. But the citric acid cycle needs free NAD⁺ and FAD to keep running, so it comes to a halt. Only glycolysis can carry on, because it regenerates its own NAD⁺ by turning pyruvate into lactate.

That is why a lack of oxygen does not halve the yield but drops it from around 30 ATP to 2 per glucose.

Compare cellular respiration and photosynthesis in a plant.

In terms of the balance sheet they run in opposite directions. Photosynthesis uses CO₂, water and light to build glucose and release oxygen; it happens in the chloroplasts and only in the light. Cellular respiration consumes glucose and oxygen, releases CO₂ and water and delivers ATP; it happens in the mitochondria, day and night.

A plant does both. In bright light photosynthesis outweighs respiration and the plant gives off oxygen on balance; at night it only respires and gives off CO₂.

The typical mistake is to write that plants "do not respire" or that photosynthesis replaces cellular respiration. Photosynthesis builds the store, cellular respiration turns it into usable energy.
