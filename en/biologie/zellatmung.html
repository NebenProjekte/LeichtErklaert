Cellular Respiration Explained Simply

Cellular respiration is the process your cells use to extract usable energy from nutrients. It takes place in the mitochondria - and is basically the opposite of photosynthesis in plants.

C&sub6;H&sub12;O&sub6; + 6 O&sub2; → 6 CO&sub2; + 6 H&sub2;O + energy glucose and oxygen are converted into carbon dioxide, water, and usable energy (ATP)

Sugar and oxygen go into the mitochondrion, usable energy (ATP) plus CO&sub2; and water come out.

💬 Mia asks Grandpa Theo

Mia

Wait - cellular respiration means you're breathing with your cells instead of your lungs?

Grandpa Theo

The two are related, but not the same. Your lungs bring oxygen into your blood and carry carbon dioxide out - that's "breathing". Cellular respiration is the chemical process inside every single cell that uses this oxygen to generate energy.

Mia

Do plants do this too, even though they photosynthesize?

Grandpa Theo

Yes, absolutely! Photosynthesis produces the sugar the plant uses as its energy store - but to actually turn that sugar into usable energy, plant cells need cellular respiration too, just like animals and fungi.

Mia

So why do we actually breathe faster when we exercise?

Grandpa Theo

Because your muscle cells suddenly need much more energy and so ramp up cellular respiration. That takes more oxygen and produces more carbon dioxide - your body responds with faster breathing and a faster heartbeat to keep the supply coming.

Even without oxygen

When your muscles briefly run short on oxygen, like during a sprint, cells switch to an oxygen-free emergency mode - which produces lactic acid, the cause of that familiar burning feeling in your muscles.

Photosynthesis and cellular respiration complement each other perfectly: one consumes CO&sub2; and produces oxygen, the other consumes oxygen and produces CO&sub2; - together they help keep the atmosphere's cycle in balance.
