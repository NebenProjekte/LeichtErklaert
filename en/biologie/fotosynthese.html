Biology - Plant Biology

Photosynthesis Explained Simply

Photosynthesis is the process by which plants use light, water and carbon dioxide to make their own energy. It does not happen in a single step but in two phases that build on each other - the light reactions and the Calvin cycle.

Light reactions → ATP + NADPH → Calvin cycle → glucose the light reactions need direct light and supply energy, the Calvin cycle uses that energy to build sugar from CO₂

Only the light reactions need direct light - the Calvin cycle "merely" uses the energy produced there and can therefore keep running briefly in the dark.

💬 Mia asks Grandpa Theo

Mia

Why does the plant need two separate steps in the first place? Why not everything at once?

Grandpa Theo

Because they are two very different jobs. In the light reactions, light energy is captured and converted into two "energy carriers" - ATP and NADPH. These carriers are then used in the Calvin cycle to build sugar molecules step by step from carbon dioxide. Two different tools for two different jobs.

Mia

Where does the oxygen we breathe actually come from? From the CO₂?

Grandpa Theo

Surprisingly, no! The oxygen comes from the water, not from the CO₂. In the light reactions, water is split to get electrons for the energy carriers - oxygen is practically a "waste product" that the plant releases into the air.

Mia

Does a plant then always grow faster the more light it gets?

Grandpa Theo

Only up to a certain point. Photosynthesis is always limited by the scarcest "ingredient" - sometimes that is the light, sometimes the amount of CO₂ in the air, sometimes the temperature. If you double the light but CO₂ is still missing, it hardly helps any more. This is called the principle of limiting factors.

You can measure it

In aquarium plants such as Canadian waterweed, you can count the rate of photosynthesis directly: the more light, the more tiny oxygen bubbles rise from the leaves per minute - a classic school experiment on limiting factors.

Almost all food chains on Earth begin with photosynthesis - plants and algae are the foundation on which practically every other living thing depends, directly or indirectly. The reverse process is cellular respiration, which releases exactly this stored energy again.

Key terms

Light reactions — at the thylakoid membrane; water is split, oxygen is released, ATP and NADPH are formed.
Calvin cycle — in the stroma; sugar is built from CO₂ with the help of ATP and NADPH.
Chlorophyll — the green pigment; it absorbs red and blue light.
Stomata — pores on the underside of the leaf through which CO₂ enters and water evaporates.
Compensation point — the light intensity at which photosynthesis and cellular respiration exactly balance each other.

The law of the minimum applies: the scarcest factor always determines the rate. If you only raise the light in a greenhouse while CO₂ is lacking, you harvest nothing extra.

Adaptations to heat and drought

The stomata are a dilemma for plants: they let CO₂ in, but also let water vapour out. Some plants have developed tricks to deal with this. Maize and sugarcane first bind the CO₂ in an intermediate product with four carbon atoms and concentrate it in special cells. This lets them keep the stomata closed for longer, and they grow well even in heat. They are called C4 plants. Cacti and other succulents open their stomata only at night, when it is cool, store the CO₂ in the form of an acid, and process it in the Calvin cycle during the day with the stomata closed. This way they lose very little water. The basic reactions stay the same in all cases; only the way the CO₂ is obtained is organised differently.

Common mistakes

The oxygen supposedly comes from the CO₂. It comes from the split water.
"Plants breathe out CO₂ at night, not during the day." They respire around the clock; during the day photosynthesis simply predominates.
The Calvin cycle as a purely night-time reaction. It also runs during the day; it just does not need light directly.
Treating all leaf cells alike. Photosynthesis takes place mainly in the palisade tissue on the upper side of the leaf.

Practice task

A piece of waterweed stands in the light and produces oxygen bubbles. When the light intensity is increased, the number of bubbles first rises, then no longer. Explain, and name one measure that raises the rate again.

Show solution

At first, light is the limiting factor: more light means more ATP and NADPH from the light reactions, and therefore more sugar and more oxygen.

Beyond a certain intensity there is an excess of light, and another factor becomes the bottleneck - usually the dissolved CO₂. More light no longer helps then.

What would work instead is adding sodium hydrogencarbonate (baking soda) to the water: it releases CO₂ and thus raises the limiting factor. A moderate increase in temperature to about 30 °C also raises enzyme activity - beyond that it causes damage.
