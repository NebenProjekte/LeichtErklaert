Percentages Explained Simply

Percent means “out of a hundred”. 20% is 20 out of 100, that is, the fraction 20/100 = 0.2. There is nothing more to it — the difficulty never lies in the calculation, but in the question of what the percentage is taken of.

Percentage value = base value · percentage rate W = G · p  |  G = W ÷ p  |  p = W ÷ G

25 out of 100 little squares are colored orange - that is 25%.

Key terms

Base value (G) — the whole, the 100%.
Percentage value (W) — the part of it, in the same unit as G.
Percentage rate (p) — the proportion, given in percent.
Percentage point — the difference between two percentages. From 4% to 6% is two percentage points, but a 50% increase.
Growth factor — 1 + p for an increase, 1 − p for a decrease. With it, several changes are simply multiplied.

The three basic problems

Which quantity you are looking for decides the formula
Looking forCalculationExample
Percentage valueW = G · p19% of €250 = 250 · 0.19 = €47.50
Base valueG = W ÷ p€47.50 is 19% — so 47.50 ÷ 0.19 = €250
Percentage ratep = W ÷ G47.50 out of 250 = 0.19 = 19%

The rule of three as a second route

If you do not like working with formulas, the rule of three (the “unitary method”) leads to the same result. It always goes through the intermediate step “1%”: you first work out how much one percent of the whole is, and then you multiply. The advantage is that you do not have to remember a formula, you only have to ask what one percent means. The calculation is a little longer, but with awkward numbers it is often easier to follow.

Looking for: 35% of €240
100% = €240 → 1% = 240 ÷ 100 = €2.40
35% = 35 · €2.40 = €84

The other way round: €84 is 35% — how big is the whole?
35% = €84 → 1% = 84 ÷ 35 = €2.40
100% = 100 · €2.40 = €240

Both routes are equivalent, and that is exactly why they are good for checking each other. If you work out a problem once with W = G · p and once with the rule of three and get the same answer both times, a calculation error is very unlikely. In exams this check usually costs only half a minute and often saves whole marks.

The growth factor saves half the work

Instead of “first work out the discount, then subtract it”, you multiply directly by the factor. A price reduction of 30% means that 70% is left — so the factor is 0.7. The big advantage shows as soon as several changes follow one another: then the factors are simply multiplied.

Price €80, first −30%, then another −10%

80 · 0.7 = €56
56 · 0.9 = €50.40

Overall: 0.7 · 0.9 = 0.63 → that is −37%, not −40%

This is exactly where the most common misconception in everyday life lies: two discounts do not add up. The second is taken from the price that has already been reduced. The same holds the other way round — plus 10% and then minus 10% gives 0.99, that is, a loss of 1%.

💬 Mia asks Grandpa Theo

Mia

In the shop it said “50% off everything” and next to it another “20% extra”. So that's 70%, right?

Grandpa Theo

Unfortunately not. 0.5 · 0.8 = 0.4 — you pay 40% and save 60%. The advert only sounds like more.

Mia

And if my report card says my average has improved by 5 percentage points?

Grandpa Theo

Percentage points are the plain difference. From 60 to 65 is five points — but only a little over eight percent increase. Newspapers mix this up all the time.

Gross, net and the classic with VAT

Net becomes gross by multiplying by 1.21 (Spain) or 1.19 (Germany). The way back is not minus 21%, but a division.

net €100 → gross 100 · 1.21 = €121
gross €121 → net 121 ÷ 1.21 = €100

wrong would be: 121 · 0.79 = €95.59  →  €4.41 too little
Common mistakes

Not dividing the percentage rate by 100. 19% is 0.19, not 19.
Adding discounts. Factors are multiplied.
Working backwards with subtraction. From a gross price you divide, you do not subtract.
Confusing percent and percentage points. In geography, politics and economics classes the difference regularly decides whether points are won or lost.
Wrong base value. “30% more than” and “30% of” are two different calculations.

Practice problem

A bicycle costs €480. In the summer sale it is reduced by 25%, and in winter by a further 20% of the already reduced price. Calculate the final price and the total discount in percent.

Show solution

480 · 0.75 = €360
360 · 0.80 = €288
Overall factor: 0.75 · 0.80 = 0.60, so a discount of 40%.
Check: 288 ÷ 480 = 0.6 ✓
It is remarkable that 25% and 20% together do not make 45% but 40% — the second reduction only acts on the remaining 75%.

Percentage change: how much has something changed?

Often what is asked for is not a proportion but a change: by how many percent has something become more expensive, cheaper or larger? For this you take the difference between the new and the old value and divide it by the old value. The old value is the base value, because it is the starting point for the calculation — it corresponds to the 100%.

The rent rises from €800 to €860.

Change: 860 − 800 = €60
Percentage change: 60 ÷ 800 = 0.075 = 7.5%

Check: 800 · 1.075 = 860 ✓

The direction matters: if a value rises by 25% and then falls by 25%, you are not back at the start. €100 first becomes €125, and 25% of €125 is €31.25, so €93.75 is left at the end. To get from €125 back to €100, a fall of 20% is enough, because 25 ÷ 125 = 0.2. The base value on the way back is simply a different one.

Why −50% can only be made up by +100%

If a share falls from €40 to €20, it has lost 50%. To get back to €40 it has to rise by €20 — and, measured against the new €20, that is a full 100%. This effect gets stronger the bigger the loss is: after −10% a gain of about 11.1% is enough (1 ÷ 0.9 ≈ 1.111), after −50% you need +100%, after −90% even +900%.

A rule of thumb for your head: you find 10% by moving the decimal point one place to the left. 5% is half of that, 20% is double. That way you can estimate almost any offer in a shop without a calculator.
