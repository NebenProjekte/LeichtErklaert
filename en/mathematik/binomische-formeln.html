Binomial Formulas Explained Simply

The binomial formulas are not new rules. They are simply the multiplied-out results of three bracket expressions that turn up all the time. If you know them, you save three steps of working every time in an exam.

(a + b)² = a² + 2ab + b²
(a − b)² = a² − 2ab + b²
(a + b)(a − b) = a² − b²
Key terms

Binomial — an expression with exactly two terms, such as a + b.
Expanding — removing the brackets: (a+b)² becomes a²+2ab+b².
Factorising — the way back: x²−9 becomes (x+3)(x−3).
Middle term — the 2ab in the middle, which is the one people forget most often.
Third formula — also called the difference of two squares; the middle term disappears.

Why the middle term is there

(a+b)² means (a+b)·(a+b). Every term in the first bracket is multiplied by every term in the second — that gives four products, two of which are equal.

(a + b)(a + b)
= a·a + a·b + b·a + b·b
= a² + ab + ab + b²
= a² + 2ab + b²

Geometrically it is a square with side length a+b. It splits into a large square a², a small square b² and two equal rectangles ab. That is where the two comes from.

The other two formulas follow from the same multiplication. For the second one you replace b by −b: (a − b)² = a² + 2a(−b) + (−b)² = a² − 2ab + b². For the third one you multiply out (a+b)(a−b) = a² − ab + ab − b². The two middle terms cancel each other, and only a² − b² remains.

How to recognise the pattern

To decide whether an expression is a binomial formula in disguise, check three things. First, are the outer terms both perfect squares? Second, is the middle term exactly twice the product of the two square roots? Third, what sign does the middle term have — plus points to the first formula, minus to the second. Take x² + 10x + 25: x² is (x)², 25 is 5², and 2 · x · 5 = 10x, which matches, so it is (x + 5)². Now take x² + 10x + 16: x² is (x)² and 16 is 4², but 2 · x · 4 = 8x, not 10x. The pattern does not fit, so this is not a binomial formula. It still factorises, though, as (x + 2)(x + 8), because 2 + 8 = 10 and 2 · 8 = 16. If the check fails, it is a sign to try another method rather than to force the formula.

The mental arithmetic trick

99² = (100 − 1)² = 10,000 − 200 + 1 = 9,801

102 · 98 = (100+2)(100−2) = 10,000 − 4 = 9,996

💬 Mia asks Grandpa Theo

Mia

Why is (a+b)² not simply a² + b²?

Grandpa Theo

Try it with numbers: (2+3)² is 25. a²+b² would be 4+9 = 13. Exactly 12 is missing — and that is the 2ab.

Mia

So I should always check with numbers first?

Grandpa Theo

Exactly. A formula that fails with two simple numbers is never correct.

Common mistakes

Forgetting the middle term. By far the most common mistake.
The sign in the second formula. Only the middle term becomes negative; b² stays positive.
Not squaring the factors. (3y)² is 9y², not 3y².
Using the third formula when both signs are the same. It only applies to (a+b)(a−b).

Practice problem

Write 9x² − 30x + 25 as a product and check your result for x = 2.

Show solution

9x² is (3x)², 25 is 5², and 2 · 3x · 5 = 30x — the middle term fits with a negative sign. So we use the second formula:
9x² − 30x + 25 = (3x − 5)²

Check with x = 2: on the left 9·4 − 60 + 25 = 36 − 60 + 25 = 1. On the right (6 − 5)² = 1 ✓

Second practice problem

Expand (2x − 3y)² and check the result with x = 1 and y = 1.

Show solution

Second formula with a = 2x and b = 3y:
(2x)² = 4x², 2 · 2x · 3y = 12xy, (3y)² = 9y²
So (2x − 3y)² = 4x² − 12xy + 9y²

Check with x = 1 and y = 1: on the left (2 − 3)² = 1. On the right 4 − 12 + 9 = 1 ✓

What they are really needed for

At school the binomial formulas first appear when you expand brackets. Their real value, however, shows in the opposite direction: factorising. If you recognise the pattern of a binomial formula in an expression, you can turn it into a product — and products can be cancelled, while sums cannot.

An example: the fraction (x² − 9) ÷ (x − 3) looks like work at first sight. But if you recognise the third binomial formula in the numerator, it becomes (x + 3)(x − 3) ÷ (x − 3), and the second factor cancels. What is left is simply x + 3. You will need exactly this shortcut all the time later on, for instance when finding limits or simplifying algebraic fractions.

Completing the square

The second big application is called completing the square, and it lies behind the whole theory of parabolas. Take x² + 6x + 1. This is not yet a binomial formula, but almost: (x + 3)² = x² + 6x + 9, so exactly 8 is missing. So you write x² + 6x + 1 = (x + 3)² − 8. The trick is always the same move: you halve the number in front of x, square it, add it and immediately subtract it again. The benefit is large, because in the new form you can read off the vertex of the parabola directly — here it is at (−3 | −8). And if you carry this route through in general for ax² + bx + c, you end up with the quadratic formula. It is not a rule that fell from the sky, but nothing other than a completed square worked through once and for all.

A third use is pure mental arithmetic. 49 · 51 can be read as (50 − 1)(50 + 1), so 2,500 − 1 = 2,499. And 102² is (100 + 2)² = 10,000 + 400 + 4 = 10,404. If you know the three formulas well, you can do such calculations in your head faster than others can type them into a calculator.

The third formula is the key to rationalising denominators: a fraction with √2 + 1 in the denominator is multiplied top and bottom by √2 − 1, and the root disappears. For example, 1 ÷ (√2 + 1) = (√2 − 1) ÷ ((√2)² − 1²) = (√2 − 1) ÷ 1 = √2 − 1.
