The Intercept Theorem Explained Simply

The intercept theorems describe what happens when two straight lines start from a common point and are cut by two parallel lines. Two triangles appear that have the same shape but different sizes. And because the shape is the same, all the lengths stand in the same ratio to one another. That single insight lets you work out the height of a tree without climbing it.

ZA : ZB = ZC : ZD = AC : BD Z is the vertex, AC and BD are the two parallel segments. All the ratios are equal
Key terms

Vertex Z — the point both rays start from.
Ray — a half-line beginning at Z.
First intercept theorem — compares segments on the same rays with one another.
Second intercept theorem — relates the parallel segments to the segments on the rays.
Similar — same shape, different size. All angles match and every side is stretched by the same factor.

Why it works

The reason is simpler than the formulas make it look. If two lines are parallel, they meet both rays at exactly the same angle — these are corresponding angles, and they are equal. The angle at the vertex Z is shared by both triangles anyway. So all three angles match, and triangles with equal angles are similar. Similar means one triangle is nothing but an enlarged copy of the other. And in a copy every length has been multiplied by the same factor — which is precisely what the intercept theorems state.

Two rays run out from the vertex Z and are cut by two parallel segments. The ratios of the sections are equal.

The first intercept theorem compares segments on the rays: if ZA is to ZB as 1 is to 3, then ZC is to ZD as 1 is to 3 as well. The second theorem brings in the parallel segments: AC to BD is then also 1 to 3. What matters is that in the first theorem you always measure from the vertex. Anyone who uses the part-segment AB instead is working with a different quantity and will get the wrong answer.

Example: ZA = 4 cm, AB = 8 cm, so ZB = 12 cm
The short parallel segment AC measures 3 cm. How long is BD?

Scale factor k = ZB / ZA = 12 / 4 = 3
BD = k · AC = 3 · 3 = 9 cm

Check using the second theorem:
ZA : ZB = AC : BD → 4 : 12 = 3 : 9 → both sides give 1/3 ✓

The most common mistake is already hiding in this small example: ZB is not 8 cm. AB is the section between the parallels, while ZB is the whole distance from the vertex, so 4 + 8 = 12 cm.

The tree in the afternoon

The classic application is measuring height with a shadow. A stick 1.50 m tall casts a shadow 2.00 m long. At the same moment a tree casts a shadow of 14.00 m. Over that distance the Sun's rays are practically parallel, so the stick and the tree form two similar triangles. That gives: tree height is to tree shadow as stick height is to stick shadow. The tree height is therefore 14.00 · 1.50 / 2.00 = 10.50 m. This is the same trick Thales is said to have used to find the height of the Great Pyramid, and it still works today with no equipment beyond a tape measure.

💬 Mia asks Grandpa Theo

Mia

Do I have to remember which theorem is the first and which is the second?

Grandpa Theo

Not for the calculation. Remember the pictures instead: one compares segments on the rays, the other compares the two parallel segments. Nobody will ask you for the number later on.

Mia

And what if the lines aren't quite parallel?

Grandpa Theo

Then the theorem no longer holds. The shadow example only works because the Sun is so far away that its rays count as parallel here. With a street lamp in the yard that is not true — there you would need a different calculation.

Common mistakes

Mixing up the sections. ZB is the distance from the vertex, AB is only the piece in between.
Not checking that the lines are parallel. No parallels, no intercept theorem.
Setting the ratio up crosswise. Both fractions must be built the same way: small over large on each side.
Mixing units. Convert centimetres and metres first, then cancel.

Practice question

A person 1.80 m tall stands 3.00 m away from a street lamp and casts a shadow 2.00 m long. How high does the lamp hang?

Show solution

Here the vertex Z is the tip of the shadow, not the lamp. Measured from the end of the shadow:
distance to the person: 2.00 m, height there 1.80 m
distance to the lamp post: 2.00 + 3.00 = 5.00 m

h / 1.80 = 5.00 / 2.00 = 2.5
h = 2.5 · 1.80 = 4.50 m

So the lamp hangs 4.50 m up. Note the difference from the Sun: here the light source is close, so it is itself the vertex of the bundle of rays — and you have to measure the distances from the shadow, not from the lamp.

Anyone who has understood the intercept theorems also understands why a photograph of a house looks exactly like the house, only smaller: the lens does nothing other than a central dilation.
