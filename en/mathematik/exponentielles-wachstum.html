Exponential Growth Explained Simply

With linear growth, the same amount is added in every step. With exponential growth, the value is multiplied by the same factor in every step. The difference looks tiny at the start and is enormous after only a few steps.

N(t) = N₀ · qt N₀ starting value · q growth factor · t number of steps
Key terms

Growth factor q — 1.05 for a 5% increase, 0.95 for a 5% decrease.
Doubling time — how long it takes to reach twice the value.
Half-life — the same for decay.
Linear growth — constant increase, the graph is a straight line.
Exponential growth — constant factor, the graph gets steeper and steeper.

The rice grain problem

Square 1: 1 grain · Square 2: 2 · Square 3: 4 · Square 4: 8 …

Square 64 alone: 263 ≈ 9.2 · 1018 grains
The whole chessboard: 264 − 1 ≈ 1.8 · 1019

At 0.03 g per grain that is about 550 billion tonnes — about a thousand times the world's annual rice harvest.

Estimating the doubling time

The rule of 70 gives a useful approximation in seconds: doubling time ≈ 70 divided by the growth rate in percent. At 7% interest, capital doubles in about ten years; at 2% it takes about 35.

The same idea works for decay. A substance with a half-life of 5 days has lost half of its amount after 5 days, three quarters after 10 days, and after 15 days only one eighth is left. Starting from 100 g, that is 100 · 0.53 = 12.5 g. Here the growth factor is q = 0.5 per half-life.

Common mistakes

Adding percentages instead of multiplying factors. Two increases of 10% make 21%, not 20%.
Getting the factor wrong. A 5% increase means q = 1.05, not 0.05.
Estimating linearly. “Double after 10 years, triple after 20” is wrong — it is four times as much.
Ignoring limits. In nature, exponential growth always ends, because space or food runs out.

Practice problem

A bacterial culture starts with 500 cells and doubles every 20 minutes. How many cells are there after 3 hours? After how long would there be a million?

Show solution

3 hours are 180 minutes, so 180 ÷ 20 = 9 doublings.
N = 500 · 29 = 500 · 512 = 256,000 cells

For a million: 1,000,000 ÷ 500 = 2,000, and 211 = 2,048 is just above that. So 11 doublings → 11 · 20 min = 220 minutes, which is 3 hours 40 minutes.
Remarkable: the culture needed three hours for its first 256,000 cells, but only twenty minutes for the next 256,000.

Why exponential growth always ends

If you keep calculating exponential growth for long enough, absurd numbers come out. A bacterial culture that doubles every 20 minutes would have more mass than the Earth after two days. That obviously does not happen — and the reason belongs to this topic just as much as the formula itself.

In reality, every kind of growth hits a limit sooner or later: the nutrient medium is used up, the space is full, the waste products poison the culture. The growth factor then does not stay constant but moves towards 1. The steep exponential curve turns into an S-curve, called logistic growth in mathematics. It starts out exponentially, is steepest in the middle and then levels off towards a maximum value, the so-called carrying capacity. Exactly the same shape describes how a disease spreads through a population, how a new product sells, or how a population grows in a limited habitat.

The calculation for compound interest

The best-known everyday example of exponential growth is compound interest, and it shows the formula in action. If you invest 2,000 euros at 4 percent per year, after n years you do not have 2,000 + n · 80 euros, but 2,000 · 1.04n. After 10 years that is 2,960 euros, after 20 years 4,382 euros and after 40 years 9,602 euros. Calculated linearly, it would only have been 5,200 euros after 40 years — the difference of over 4,400 euros is pure compound-interest effect. The rule of 70 lets you estimate this in your head: 70 divided by 4 gives about 17.5 years of doubling time, and 40 years is a little over two doublings, so roughly four to five times the starting amount. The exact calculation confirms it: 1.0440 is about 4.8. This is precisely why the same formula is so powerful in both directions — in saving it works in the investor's favour, with debts and loans it works against them.

For exams it is worth keeping the two cases cleanly apart: as long as nothing slows the growth, N(t) = N0 · qt holds. As soon as a limit comes into play, the exponential function is only an approximation for the early phase — and anyone who uses it beyond that ends up with bacteria as heavy as the Earth.
