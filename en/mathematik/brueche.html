Fractions Explained Simply

A fraction describes a part of a whole. It is not a second kind of number alongside the whole numbers, just another way of writing a division that does not come out evenly. That is exactly why the same arithmetic rules apply to fractions as to everything else — you only have to understand them properly once.

Numerator / Denominator the denominator says how many equal parts the whole is divided into · the numerator says how many of those parts you mean

With 3/4 a pizza is cut into four equally sized pieces and you take three of them. The words equally sized matter: three out of four pieces torn off at random are not a fraction, just a mess.

Key terms

Numerator — the top number, it counts the parts taken.
Denominator — the bottom number, it names the size of the parts.
Expanding — multiplying numerator and denominator by the same number. The value stays the same, the parts get smaller and more numerous.
Simplifying — dividing numerator and denominator by the same number. The reverse of expanding.
Proper fraction — numerator smaller than denominator, the value is below 1.
Improper fraction — numerator greater than or equal to the denominator, e.g. 7/4.
Mixed number — the same thing as a whole number plus a remainder, 7/4 = 1 3/4.
Common denominator — the shared denominator several fractions are brought to. The lowest common multiple is the most convenient one.

Expanding and simplifying do not change the value

This is the heart of everything. If you cut a pizza into eight pieces instead of four and take twice as many pieces, you have exactly the same amount on your plate. In arithmetic: you multiply numerator and denominator by the same number, that is, by a hidden 1.

1/2 = 2/4 = 4/8 = 50/100

1/2 · 2/2 = 2/4   →   2/2 is 1, so nothing changes
12/18 ÷ 6/6 = 2/3   →   simplified by 6

A fraction is called fully simplified when numerator and denominator share no divisor other than 1. At school this is almost always expected — it is the most common deduction on otherwise correct answers.

The four operations

Working with fractions at a glance
OperationRuleExample
Addingmake the denominators equal, then add the numerators1/2 + 1/4 = 2/4 + 1/4 = 3/4
Subtractingmake the denominators equal, then subtract the numerators5/6 − 1/3 = 5/6 − 2/6 = 3/6 = 1/2
Multiplyingnumerator times numerator, denominator times denominator2/3 · 3/5 = 6/15 = 2/5
Dividingmultiply by the reciprocal3/4 ÷ 1/2 = 3/4 · 2/1 = 6/4 = 3/2

Notice: adding needs a common denominator, multiplying does not. That confuses a lot of people. The reason is easy to picture — when adding, the pieces have to be the same size or you cannot count them together. When multiplying you take a part of a part, and the new piece size appears by itself.

💬 Mia asks Grandpa Theo

Mia

And how do I add 1/2 + 1/4 together? The denominators are different!

Grandpa Theo

That is exactly why you first bring them to the same denominator. 1/2 is the same as 2/4 — you simply expand by 2.

Mia

Ah! So 2/4 + 1/4 = 3/4?

Grandpa Theo

Exactly. And when multiplying you do not need that at all — there you just multiply top and bottom straight through.

Why dividing means multiplying by the reciprocal

The rule looks like a conjuring trick but it is only a question. "How often does 1/2 fit into 3/4?" Half a piece fits into three quarters one and a half times. And that is precisely what the calculation gives: 3/4 · 2/1 = 6/4 = 3/2 = 1.5. The reciprocal turns the question "how many parts" into "how often does it fit".

Common mistakes

Adding the denominators too. 1/2 + 1/3 is not 2/5. If it were, 1/2 + 1/2 would give 2/4 = 0.5 — but two halves make one whole.
Expanding only one of the two. Turning 1/2 into 2/4 means touching numerator and denominator.
Not simplifying at the end. 6/8 is arithmetically right but incomplete in a test — 3/4 is what is wanted.
Multiplying mixed numbers wrongly. 1 1/2 · 2 is not 1 · 2 and 1/2 · 2 side by side. Convert to 3/2 first, then calculate.

Practice question

A recipe for 6 people needs 3/4 of a litre of milk. You are cooking for 4 people. How much milk do you need? Give the answer as a fully simplified fraction and in millilitres.

Show solution

For 4 people instead of 6 you need the fraction 4/6 = 2/3 of the amount.
3/4 · 2/3 = 6/12 = 1/2 litre, so 500 ml.
Check by the other route: 3/4 of a litre is 750 ml, which is 125 ml per person. Four people need 4 · 125 ml = 500 ml. Both routes give the same answer — that kind of cross-check is worth more in an exam than any memorised rule.

A rule of thumb for everyday life: a fraction bar is a division sign. If you are unsure, type 3 ÷ 4 into a calculator and you get 0.75 — the same value, written another way.

When a fraction never ends

Some fractions never come out exactly as a decimal. 1/3 gives 0.3333… with infinitely many threes. That is not a rounding error but a property of the decimal system: only fractions whose denominator breaks down entirely into twos and fives produce a terminating decimal. 1/8 = 0.125 works because 8 = 2·2·2, while 1/6 repeats because there is a 3 in the denominator. This is exactly why a fraction is often the more precise way of writing a value: 1/3 is exact, 0.333 is merely close. In an exam, leaving an answer as a simplified fraction is therefore usually safer than converting it to a decimal and rounding.
