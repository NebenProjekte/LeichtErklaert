Area and Volume Formulas Explained Simply

Whether circle, sphere or cylinder, the formulas for circumference, area, volume and surface area are more closely connected than you might think. All of them only need the radius r (and, for the cylinder, the height h as well). Here you will find everything you need for a maths exam on the topic, including the mistakes that happen most often.

Circumference of a circle: C = 2 × π × r r is the radius - half of the diameter d!
Area of a circle: A = π × r²
Volume of a sphere: V = 4⁄3 × π × r³
Surface area of a sphere: S = 4 × π × r²
Volume of a cylinder: V = π × r² × h
Surface area of a cylinder: S = 2 × π × r × (r + h) Base + top + lateral surface

💬 Mia asks Grandpa Theo

Mia

Why does the sphere have almost the same formula as the circle, just with r³ instead of r²?

Grandpa Theo

Because a sphere is basically a 3D circle! For an area (2D) you count r × r, and for a volume (3D) one more dimension is simply added: r × r × r.

Mia

And the cylinder?

Grandpa Theo

A cylinder is simply a circle that is “pulled” upwards by the height h - that is why its volume is the area of the circle times the height: π × r² × h.

Mia

What is actually the most common mistake in problems like these?

Grandpa Theo

Mixing up radius and diameter! If the problem gives the diameter (e.g. d = 10 cm), you first have to divide by 2 to get the radius (r = 5 cm). If you accidentally calculate with the diameter instead of the radius, the area comes out four times too large and the volume even eight times too large!

Mia

How do you even arrive at the surface area formula for the cylinder? It looks complicated.

Grandpa Theo

It is not that hard: imagine peeling the label off a tin can and laying it flat - that is a rectangle! Its width is the circumference of the circle (2×π×r) and its height is h. On top of that come the two round lids at the top and bottom (2×π×r²). Together that gives exactly the formula.

Mia

Show me one more complete worked example with everything together!

Grandpa Theo

Let's take a bicycle front wheel with a diameter of d = 60 cm, so r = 30 cm. The circumference is C = 2×π×30 ≈ 188.5 cm - that is the distance the wheel covers in exactly one turn! The area would be A = π×30² ≈ 2,827 cm².

Example 1: a complete cylinder (tin can, r = 3.5 cm, h = 12 cm)

Volume: V = π × 3.5² × 12 ≈ 462 cm³
Surface area: S = 2 × π × 3.5 × (3.5+12) = 2 × π × 3.5 × 15.5 ≈ 340.9 cm²

Example 2: a complete sphere (football, r ≈ 11 cm)

Volume: V = 4⁄3 × π × 11³ ≈ 5,575 cm³ (over 5.5 litres of air!)
Surface area: S = 4 × π × 11² ≈ 1,521 cm² - that roughly corresponds to the area of leather the ball is stitched together from.

Example 3: for you to work out

A pizza has a diameter of 32 cm. What is its area?
Careful, this is a trap: first divide d = 32 cm by 2! r = 16 cm, then A = π × 16² ≈ 804 cm²

When the length doubles

One of the most useful insights is not in the individual formulas at all, but in the way they are built: how does the result change if you multiply all lengths by the same factor?

In an area the radius is squared, in a volume it is cubed. So if you double the radius, the area becomes four times as large and the volume eight times as large. If you triple it, you get nine times and twenty-seven times. This holds not only for circles and spheres but for any shape, as long as you enlarge it evenly - including people, animals and pizzas.

The large pizza is almost always the better buy

A pizza with a 26 cm diameter has a radius of 13 cm and therefore an area of π · 13² ≈ 531 cm². One with a 32 cm diameter has a radius of 16 cm and an area of π · 16² ≈ 804 cm². The diameter is only about 23 percent larger, but the area is over 50 percent larger. If the small one costs 9 euros and the large one 11 euros, you pay about 1.7 cents per square centimetre for one and about 1.4 cents for the other - the large one is clearly cheaper. The comparison with two small pizzas is even clearer: two times 531 cm² is 1,062 cm², while a single one with a 40 cm diameter has π · 20² ≈ 1,257 cm². One large pizza beats two small ones, although its diameter is only about one and a half times as large.

The same relationship also explains why small animals have to eat so much. Heat is lost through the surface but produced in the volume. If an animal becomes twice as long, its surface area grows fourfold, but its volume eightfold - so the ratio of surface to volume is halved. Large animals therefore cool down more slowly. A mouse has to eat almost constantly, while an elephant gets by on comparatively little food per kilogram of body weight. And the same relationship is the reason why chopped vegetables cook faster than whole ones: when you cut them up, the surface area grows a lot while the volume stays the same.

π (pi) is approximately 3.14159 - the formulas are exact only for perfect circles, spheres and cylinders; real objects are always just approximations of them. In exams, always watch the units: areas are given in cm²/m², volumes in cm³/m³.
