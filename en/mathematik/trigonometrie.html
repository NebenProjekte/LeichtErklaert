Sine, Cosine and Tangent Explained Simply

Trigonometry connects angles with lengths. As soon as a triangle has a right angle, one angle and one side are enough to work out everything else. Surveying, navigation and every resolution of forces in physics live off exactly this.

sin α = opposite / hypotenuse
cos α = adjacent / hypotenuse
tan α = opposite / adjacent
Key terms

Hypotenuse — the side opposite the right angle, always the longest.
Opposite side — lies across from the angle under consideration.
Adjacent side — lies next to the angle under consideration.
Sine, cosine, tangent — ratios of sides, not lengths. That is why they have no units.
Inverse trigonometric functions — the reverse, on a calculator sin⁻¹, cos⁻¹, tan⁻¹. The ratio is turned into the angle.

Why the ratios are fixed

Two right triangles with the same angle are similar to each other — one is just an enlarged version of the other. With an enlargement all sides grow by the same factor, so every ratio of sides stays unchanged. That is why the sine depends only on the angle and not on the size of the triangle.

At 45° sine and cosine are equal — the triangle is symmetrical. At 90° the tangent has no value, because the adjacent side shrinks to zero and you cannot divide by zero.

A tree casts a shadow 12 m long, and the sun is 34° above the horizon.

tan 34° = height ÷ 12
height = 12 · tan 34° = 12 · 0.6745 = 8.09 m

💬 Mia asks Grandpa Theo

Mia

How do I know whether I need sine or cosine?

Grandpa Theo

Look at which sides are involved. Opposite and hypotenuse means sine, adjacent and hypotenuse means cosine, and the two legs mean tangent.

Mia

And if I am looking for the angle?

Grandpa Theo

Then you work out the ratio and press sin⁻¹ or tan⁻¹. Just make sure the calculator is set to DEG and not to RAD.

Common mistakes

Calculator in radian mode. If the display shows RAD instead of DEG, all results are wrong. Check: sin 30° must give 0.5.
Mixing up the legs. “Opposite” and “adjacent” always refer to the angle under consideration, not to the right angle.
Reading sin⁻¹ as 1/sin. It is the inverse function, not the reciprocal.
Applying it to oblique triangles. Without a right angle you need the law of sines or the law of cosines.

Practice problem

A ramp climbs a height of 0.75 m over a length of 9.00 m (measured along the ramp). Calculate the angle of slope. For wheelchairs a slope of at most 6% is permitted — is the ramp permitted?

Show solution

The height is the opposite side, the ramp length is the hypotenuse → sine.
sin α = 0.75 ÷ 9.00 = 0.0833
α = sin⁻¹(0.0833) = 4.78°

The slope in percent is the tangent: horizontal distance = √(9.00² − 0.75²) = 8.969 m, so tan α = 0.75 ÷ 8.969 = 0.0836 → 8.4%.
So the ramp is above the limit of 6% and is not permitted. For 6% it could rise by at most 0.06 m per metre of horizontal distance — for a height of 0.75 m about 12.5 m of horizontal distance would be needed.

The unit circle

In a right triangle, sine and cosine are only defined for angles between 0 and 90 degrees — larger ones simply do not fit inside. The unit circle solves this problem and is therefore the next step after the ratios of sides.

You draw a circle with radius 1 around the origin of the coordinate system. Then you measure the angle from the positive x-axis, counter-clockwise, and see where the arm of the angle meets the circle. This point has the coordinates (cos α | sin α). That is not a new definition but the same as before: because the hypotenuse here is exactly 1 long, the opposite and adjacent sides are directly equal to the sine and cosine. Only now this view works for every angle — also for 150 degrees, for 270 degrees or for negative values.

What you can read off immediately

The unit circle explains several things at a glance. First, the signs: in the second quadrant the x-coordinate is negative, so the cosine is negative there, while the sine stays positive. Second, the values on the axes: at 0 degrees the point is at (1 | 0), at 90 degrees at (0 | 1), at 180 degrees at (−1 | 0). From this it follows immediately that sin 0° = 0, cos 90° = 0, and why the tangent is not defined at 90 degrees — you would have to divide by zero. Third, the periodicity: after one full turn you are back at the starting point, so all values repeat every 360 degrees. And fourth, the best-known relationship in trigonometry simply drops out: the point lies on a circle of radius 1, so by the Pythagorean theorem sin²α + cos²α = 1. This equation is often learned by heart — yet it is nothing other than the circle itself.

If you let the point travel slowly once around the circle in your mind, and record only its height, you get exactly the sine curve. That completes the transition to oscillations — sound, light and alternating current are all described with the same function.

The same functions also describe oscillations: sound, light and alternating current are represented as sine curves. The detour via the triangle is only the starting point.
