Sports Theory - Upper Secondary

Hypertrophy explained simply

Hypertrophy is the increase in muscle cross-section through enlargement of the existing fibres. According to current knowledge, no new fibres are formed to any notable extent in humans - hyperplasia has been demonstrated in animal experiments, but in humans it is disputed and negligible for practice.

What grows above all are the myofibrils, that is, the contractile elements. To be distinguished from this is sarcoplasmic enlargement, in which the non-contractile part increases - it raises the circumference but hardly the strength.

Stimulus → fatigue → recovery → adaptation If one link fails, the adaptation does not occur

The supercompensation model

The model is a simplification. It describes a single stimulus acting on a single system – in reality, adaptations proceed at different speeds for muscles, tendons and the nervous system.

For the exam it is important to know the model and at the same time be able to put it in perspective. Points of criticism that earn full marks:

  • It assumes one performance state, although muscles, tendons, the nervous system and energy stores recover at different speeds.
  • It does not explain why several training sessions in a fatigued state (block training) also work.
  • The fitness-fatigue model is considered more accurate today: performance results from the superposition of a slowly decaying fitness component and a quickly decaying fatigue component.

What happens in the cell

Muscle fibres are multinucleated. Each nucleus supplies only a limited region, the so-called myonuclear domain. If the fibre is to become thicker, it needs additional nuclei - and these come from satellite cells, muscular stem cells at the edge of the fibre.

These nuclei are retained even when the muscle shrinks again during a training break. This is the cellular basis of muscle memory: whoever has built muscle once builds it up again faster after a break.

Control runs through the mTOR pathway, which ramps up protein biosynthesis. What matters is the net protein balance: synthesis minus breakdown. After a session, the rate of synthesis is elevated for about 24 to 48 hours.

Key terms
Hypertrophy
Increase in cross-section through enlargement of existing muscle fibres.
Hyperplasia
Increase in the number of fibres; disputed in humans.
Myofibril
Contractile unit of the muscle fibre made of actin and myosin.
Satellite cell
Muscular stem cell that supplies the fibre with additional nuclei.
Myonuclear domain
Cell volume that a single nucleus can supply.
Net protein balance
Difference between protein synthesis and breakdown.
Supercompensation
Model-based adaptation beyond the baseline level after recovery.
Common exam mistake

Presenting supercompensation as a fact instead of a model. Whoever merely reproduces the model gets basic marks. Whoever names its limits and mentions the fitness-fatigue model shows evaluation skills - that is the difference between a good and a very good grade.

"Muscles grow during sleep" as an explanation. Correct, but incomplete. What is asked for are the stimulus mechanism, the satellite cells and the elevated rate of protein synthesis over 24 to 48 hours.

How to phrase it in an exam

"The training stimulus disturbs homeostasis and leads to a temporary reduction in performance. In the recovery phase an adaptation beyond the baseline level takes place, which is called supercompensation. The model must, however, be put in perspective insofar as it assumes a single performance state, whereas the systems involved recover at different speeds."

Exam question

A student trains the same muscle group on six days of the week and sees no gain after four weeks. Explain the problem with the help of the supercompensation model and assess how informative this model is for the present case.

Show expected answer

First, the sequence stimulus - fatigue - recovery - adaptation is to be presented and the optimal next stimulus placed in the range of supercompensation, which for hypertrophy training is after about 48 to 72 hours. With daily training, the new stimulus hits during the fatigue phase; the net protein balance stays negative, and stagnation or overtraining results. For the assessment it should be added that the model simplifies: it assumes a single performance state, although muscles, tendons and the nervous system have different recovery times. The fitness-fatigue model explains the case more precisely, since here the persistent fatigue component overlays the fitness component. As a consequence, a reduction to two to three sessions per muscle group per week with an unchanged weekly volume is to be derived.
