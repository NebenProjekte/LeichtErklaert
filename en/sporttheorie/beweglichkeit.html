Sports Theory - Upper Secondary

Flexibility explained simply

Flexibility is the ability to perform movements with the required range of motion. It is not a single quantity but is made up of two independent components - and exactly this distinction is asked for in exams:

The two components of flexibility
ComponentDetermined byTrainable?
Joint mobilityStructure of the joint: shape of the joint surfaces, capsule, ligamentshardly - fixed by anatomy
StretchabilityMuscles, tendons, fasciae, neural protective tensionyes - this is where all stretching training starts

In addition, one distinguishes active flexibility (reached by your own muscle force) from passive flexibility (reached by an external force, gravity or a partner). The difference between the two is called the range-of-motion reserve. If it is large, what is missing is not stretchability but strength at the end of the range.

Range-of-motion reserve = passive − active flexibility A large reserve is a strength problem, not a stretching problem

The neural brake

The limiting factor in stretching is rarely the length of the muscle. Usually it is the protective tension, controlled by two receptors with opposite effects.

The two receptors work against each other. Fast stretching wakes up the spindle and the muscle resists; long, calm holding lets the Golgi organ gain the upper hand.

Muscle spindle and stretch reflex

The muscle spindle lies parallel to the fibres and measures length and the speed of stretch. During a fast stretch it triggers a monosynaptic reflex via the Ia afferent: the α-motor neuron fires and the muscle contracts. This is why jerky stretching is counterproductive - you stretch against your own reflex.

Golgi tendon organ and autogenic inhibition

The Golgi tendon organ lies in series at the muscle-tendon junction and measures tension. When tension stays high, it inhibits the α-motor neuron via an interneuron - the muscle lets go. This autogenic inhibition is the reason why a stretch gives way after about 20 to 30 seconds.

Stretch tolerance

The explanation that dominates today for gains in flexibility: in the short term the tissue does not become longer, but the tolerance of the stretch sensation rises. Real structural adaptations - for example more sarcomeres in series - need weeks to months of regular training.

Common exam mistake

Recommending static stretching before competition. Long static stretching of more than 60 seconds per muscle acutely lowers explosive strength measurably - studies show losses in the low double-digit percentage range for about an hour. Before jumps, sprints and throws, dynamic stretching belongs in the warm-up, static stretching in a separate session or at the end.

"Stretching prevents muscle soreness and injuries." Reliable evidence is missing for both. Anyone who states it as a fact loses marks.

Mixing up joint mobility and stretchability. Stretching does not change the shape of a hip joint.

Key terms
Flexibility
Ability to perform movements with the required range of motion.
Joint mobility
The anatomically determined part of flexibility.
Stretchability
The trainable part, determined by muscles, tendons and the nervous system.
Range-of-motion reserve
Difference between passive and active flexibility.
Muscle spindle
Receptor lying in parallel that senses length and speed of stretch.
Golgi tendon organ
Receptor lying in series that senses muscle tension.
Autogenic inhibition
Inhibition of the muscle's own contraction via the Golgi tendon organ.
Reciprocal inhibition
Inhibition of the antagonist when the agonist contracts.
How to phrase it in an exam

"The limiting factor is predominantly neural. During a fast stretch, the muscle spindle activates the α-motor neuron monosynaptically via the Ia afferent, so that the muscle contracts reflexively. If, on the other hand, the tension is held for about 20 seconds, autogenic inhibition by the Golgi tendon organ predominates, and stretchability increases in the short term."

Exam question

Before a competition, a coach has her long jumpers stretch statically for ten minutes. Assess this approach from a sports science point of view and develop a justified alternative.

Show expected answer

The approach must be judged critically. Long static stretching leads to an acute reduction of explosive and reactive strength, which is decisive for performance at take-off. Causes to name: reduced muscle-tendon stiffness, so that less elastic energy is stored and transferred in the stretch-shortening cycle, and reduced neural activation. Since a short ground contact and a high impulse are decisive in the long jump, the measure works against the target movement. As an alternative, a warm-up should be developed with general warming up, followed by dynamic stretching in the sport-specific range of motion, progressive build-up runs and take-off drills. Static stretching can be moved to a separate session or to the end of training if an improvement in stretchability is intended.
