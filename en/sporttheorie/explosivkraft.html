Sports Theory - Upper Secondary

Explosive strength and speed strength explained simply

Speed strength is the ability to produce the largest possible impulse in the time available. The decisive difference from maximal strength lies in the word "available": in many sports the time that would be needed to build up full maximal strength is simply not there.

Building up maximal strength takes, depending on the muscle, 300 to 400 milliseconds. Ground contact during a take-off lasts about 200 milliseconds, in a sprint stride only 80 to 100. Whatever has not been built up by then does not enter the movement.

Impulse = ∫ F dt The integral of force over time - the area under the force-time curve

The area under the curve is the impulse. Whatever lies to the right of the 200-millisecond line comes too late for the take-off - which is why the rise decides, not the final value.

The three characteristic values of the force-time curve

Maximal strengthHighest value reachedfrom approx. 300 msWeightlifting, powerlifting

An athlete can have high maximal strength and still a flat rise curve. This is exactly what explains why the strongest person in a group is rarely the best jumper.

Reactive strength and the stretch-shortening cycle

Reactive strength is a special form of speed strength: it uses a stretch immediately beforehand to reinforce the shortening that follows. Three mechanisms work together:

  • Elastic energy storage in tendon and titin, comparable to a stretched rubber band
  • Stretch reflex via the muscle spindle, which switches on additional motor units
  • Pre-tension - the muscle is already activated when the shortening begins and does not have to build up force first

All three methods have one thing in common: they are stopped before fatigue. Anyone who trains speed strength to muscular failure is training something else - movement speed is the training goal, not the number of repetitions.

Worked example: rate of force development

Athlete A reaches 1,800 N after 300 ms.
Athlete B reaches 1,500 N after 150 ms.

rate of force development = ΔF ÷ Δt
A: 1,800 N ÷ 0.30 s = 6,000 N/s
B: 1,500 N ÷ 0.15 s = 10,000 N/s

Time available at take-off: 200 ms
A has then built up about 1,200 N
B already has the full 1,500 N

A is stronger in maximal terms but jumps worse. This is the core of speed strength and a rewarding exam example.

Key terms
Speed strength
Ability to produce the largest possible impulse in the time available.
Impulse
Integral of force over time; equals the change in momentum.
Starting strength
Force value after the first 30 milliseconds.
Explosive strength
Maximal rate of force development dF/dt in the steepest section of the curve.
Reactive strength
Speed strength using a stretch-shortening cycle.
Amortisation phase
Reversal phase between stretch and shortening; the shorter it is, the less energy is lost.
Post-activation potentiation
Briefly increased speed strength performance after a heavy preloading stimulus.
Common exam mistake

Equating speed strength with speed. Speed is an independent ability and describes movement frequency; speed strength describes the course of force against a resistance.

Calling the impulse a force. The impulse is a product of force and time and has the unit newton-second. Anyone who writes only "a lot of force" loses the decisive point.

How to phrase it in an exam

"Since ground contact at take-off lasts only about 200 ms, whereas the full development of maximal strength needs 300 to 400 ms, it is not the maximum value but the rate of force development that determines performance. The impulse realised corresponds to the area under the force-time curve within the time available."

Exam question

Two volleyball players have the same maximal strength but differ clearly in jump height. Analyse possible causes using the force-time curve and derive a suitable training method.

Show expected answer

The answer should recognise that the same maximal strength with a different rate of force development leads to different impulses within the roughly 200 ms of ground contact. The weaker jumper reaches only part of his maximal strength in the time available; his strength deficit in the relevant time window is larger. Causes to name: lower recruitment speed, lower impulse frequency at the start of the movement, a less favourable fibre type distribution, and an amortisation phase that is too long in the stretch-shortening cycle. The training method to derive is the explosive strength method at 50-70% intensity with maximal movement speed, supplemented by plyometric exercises with short ground contact; alternatively the contrast method using post-activation potentiation. Important for full marks: the reason why the load is ended before fatigue.
