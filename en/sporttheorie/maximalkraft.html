Sports Theory - Upper Secondary

Maximal strength explained simply

Maximal strength is the greatest force the neuromuscular system can produce in a voluntary contraction against an insurmountable resistance. The word "voluntary" matters in exams: it separates maximal strength from absolute strength.

Absolute strength additionally includes the autonomically protected reserves - the share the body normally keeps locked to protect tendons, ligaments and bones. It is released only under mortal fear, hypnosis or doping. The difference between the two is called the strength deficit and amounts to roughly 30% in untrained people but only about 10% in highly trained athletes.

Strength deficit = absolute strength − maximal strength The smaller the deficit, the better the voluntary activation

This is exactly why a beginner gains strength so quickly in the first weeks: he does not build new tissue, he reduces his strength deficit.

The four determining factors

1. Muscle cross-section

What counts is the physiological cross-section, that is, the cut surface perpendicular to the direction of the fibres - not the anatomical circumference. In pennate muscles it is considerably larger than the circumference suggests. As a rule of thumb, a muscle produces about 30 to 40 N of force per cm² of cross-section.

2. Intramuscular coordination

The interplay within a single muscle, controlled by three mechanisms:

  • Recruitment - how many motor units are switched on
  • Rate coding - how quickly the impulses follow one another; from about 50 Hz the single twitches fuse into tetanus
  • Synchronisation - how simultaneously the units fire

Recruitment follows Henneman's size principle: small, slow units are activated first, large, fast units last. It follows directly why high loads or training to exhaustion are needed to reach the type II fibres at all.

3. Intermuscular coordination

The interplay of several muscles: agonist, synergists and the inhibition of the antagonist. It is highly movement-specific - whoever trains the squat becomes stronger in the squat, not automatically in the leg press.

4. Fibre type distribution

Largely determined by genetics. What can mainly be trained is the shift between IIa and IIx, not the change between type I and type II.

The bar lengths show the relative strength of each property. Type I works for a long time at low force, type IIx delivers a lot of force but tires within seconds. Type IIa lies in between and can be shifted in both directions through training.

The time course of the adaptations is a popular exam topic: in the first two to four weeks, almost only neural adaptation is at work. A measurable increase in cross-section begins only after about four to six weeks.

Worked example: relative strength

Relative strength puts maximal strength in proportion to body weight. It decides performance in every sport in which the athlete's own body has to be moved.

Athlete A: 60 kg body weight, 120 kg squat
Athlete B: 90 kg body weight, 160 kg squat

relative strength = maximal strength ÷ body weight
A: 120 ÷ 60 = 2.0
B: 160 ÷ 90 = 1.78

B lifts more in absolute terms, A is relatively stronger. In gymnastics or climbing A has the advantage, in open-class weightlifting B does.

Key terms
Maximal strength
Greatest force in a voluntary contraction against an insurmountable resistance.
Absolute strength
Maximal strength plus the autonomically protected reserves.
Strength deficit
Difference between absolute and maximal strength; a measure of how fully the activation is exploited.
Motor unit
One motor neuron together with all the muscle fibres it supplies.
Recruitment
Switching on motor units according to the size principle.
Rate coding
Increase of the impulse rate up to tetanic fusion.
Synchronisation
Simultaneous firing of several motor units.
Common exam mistake

"Maximal strength depends on muscle cross-section." This is incomplete and costs marks. Cross-section is one of four factors. If you leave out the coordination components, you cannot explain afterwards why beginners get stronger without visible muscle growth.

Also often confused: absolute strength is not the maximum you can lift. It is exactly the other way round - it is the theoretical value including the locked reserve.

How to phrase it in an exam

"Maximal strength is determined by four factors: the physiological muscle cross-section, the intramuscular coordination with recruitment, rate coding and synchronisation, the intermuscular coordination, and the fibre type distribution. In the early phase of strength training, neural adaptations dominate, which is why strength gains occur without hypertrophy."

Exam question

Explain why a beginner moves clearly more weight after four weeks of strength training although the muscle circumference is unchanged. Justify your answer with the determining factors of maximal strength.

Show expected answer

The answer should separate neural from morphological adaptations. In the first two to four weeks the intramuscular coordination improves: more motor units are recruited, the impulse frequency rises, and the units fire more synchronously. In addition the intermuscular coordination improves, because the movement pattern and the inhibition of the antagonist become more economical. Both reduce the strength deficit without changing the muscle cross-section. Hypertrophy is not yet measurable in this period, since protein synthesis leads to an increase in cross-section only after about four to six weeks. For full marks, at least three of the mechanisms named should be identified and the time course should be justified.
